---

Bitcoin builds the wealth. Cash flow is the engine underneath it.

Your surplus is the money left over each month after taxes, living expenses, and debt payments. It decides whether you can keep buying Bitcoin, keep holding through a downturn, or get forced to sell at the bottom. In 2022 Bitcoin dropped 77%, and the people who got hurt most were the ones whose cash flow forced them to sell into it.

### The surplus walk

Let's say we have a couple in Texas. He's 45, she's 43, two kids. Their year walks like this:

| Step | Amount |
|---|---|
| Gross income | $190,000 |
| Taxes (federal + payroll, no state) | -$40,000 |
| Living expenses | -$80,000 |
| Debt payments (mortgage + car) | -$22,000 |
| **Surplus** | **$48,000 / year (~$4,000/mo)** |

That $4,000 a month is the budget every later module has to work with. Until you've run this on your own numbers, your plan is running on a guess.

Notice what is **not** subtracted: retirement contributions. The 401(k) and the Roth IRA are not expenses, they are decisions, and Module 3 is where you make them. Surplus is money that is not already committed to taxes, living, or debt. Where it goes is the next question, not this one.

### The one input people get wrong

Your spending number is your after-tax living expenses. Not gross income. Not debt payments.

Enter your paycheck ($190,000) instead of your actual spending ($80,000), and the plan sizes your retirement to fund $110,000/yr of spending that doesn't exist. The stack it asks you to build is more than double what your life needs. Every projection downstream is wrong.

The app separates these into four rows: **Income**, **Taxes**, **Living**, **Debt payments**.

### Keep, Cut, Reduce

The goal is to find dollars that aren't buying you much happiness, safety, or future freedom.

Label every expense with one of three:

- **KEEP.** Essentials and things clearly worth the money. Housing, groceries, minimum debt payments.
- **CUT.** Eliminate. Unused subscriptions, impulse buys, lifestyle creep. The couple's first pass found $85/mo of subscriptions nobody had opened in 90 days.
- **REDUCE.** Keep it, but lower it. Insurance, phone, internet, memberships. One call that saves $40/mo is $480/yr, off a call you made once.

### The three big levers

After the easy cuts, your largest fixed costs are where the real surplus hides:

- **Housing.** Downsize, switch rentals, take a roommate.
- **Car.** Buy used. Sell a car to kill a big payment.
- **Location.** Cost of living plus state taxes add up every year you stay.

A $500/mo housing change is $6,000/yr, every year, without another decision.

In 2017 Didi Taihuttu sold his family home in the Netherlands and moved his wife and three kids into a campsite, converting the proceeds into Bitcoin at ~$900/coin. That stack is worth several million today, and he could buy back a comparable house for a small fraction of the coins he bought. Not a recommendation. But it shows the direction the housing lever pulls.

### Two spending numbers, not one

You need two:

- What you normally spend.
- What you could survive on if things got ugly. Your bare-bones floor.

The couple's floor walk: $2,300 housing + $360 car + $1,200 groceries + $500 utilities and phone + $400 insurance + $240 gas and kids = **$5,000/mo**.

Their normal is $6,700/mo, plus $1,850/mo of debt payments, so about $8,550/mo all in. Floor is $5,000/mo all in. The $3,550/mo gap is travel, eating out, and extras that pause in a bad year.

The bare-bones number is what your cash reserve gets sized off of.

### The withholding lever

Withholding can be off in two directions:

- **Over-withhold.** A $4,800 spring refund is $400/mo of your own surplus you spent all year pretending you didn't have.
- **Under-withhold.** Real risk for two-earner households: $150k plus $40k gets taxed together, and neither employer withholds as if it knew about the other paycheck.

Check what you're withholding against what you'll actually owe.

### Route new income the day it lands

Every raise gets a decision the same day. A $5,000 raise is ~$400/mo. Route it before it becomes lifestyle. Eighteen months of "I'll figure it out later" and the raise is gone.

- Write down both spending numbers: normal and bare-bones.
- Enter the bare-bones number into the reserve settings in the app.


## 3.2 Size the reserve to your life

**By the end of this lesson, you can:** *(not rendering in the app)*

- Size a reserve using bare-bones × target months
- Adjust target months up or down based on six factors
- Understand how the reserve's job changes at retirement

---

Your reserve is cash you hold on purpose to keep from selling Bitcoin at the wrong time.

The couple holds 1.75 Bitcoin. In 2022, Bitcoin dropped 77% peak to bottom. If a job loss had landed in that window and they held too little cash, the mortgage doesn't pause. They're selling Bitcoin at a 77% discount to make the house payment.

Hold too much cash, though, and the drag costs the plan for years. This lesson sizes the middle.

For retirement, 18 months is the planning default, because the first few years after you stop working are when sequence risk is most dangerous. It is not a prediction of how long the next Bitcoin recovery takes.

### The formula

**Bare-bones essentials × target months = reserve target.**

The bare-bones number is your floor from the surplus lesson, not your normal spending. In a real emergency you'd drop to the floor anyway, so sizing off normal spending buys insurance for a lifestyle you'd have cut. Sizing off the floor buys you about a third more time for the same dollars.

The app has four fields under **Cash Flow → Reserve settings**:

- **Bare-bones essentials.** Your floor number.
- **Target months.** How many months of the floor to hold.
- **Reserve basis.** Floor or normal spending. Default: floor.
- **Monthly build cap.** How fast the routing fills it.

### Target months by situation

| Situation | Target |
|---|---|
| Dual income, stable work, no dependents | ~3 months |
| Baseline for most households | ~6 months |
| Single income, self-employed, variable pay | 12+ months |

Six factors move the number up or down:

- **Income instability.** Variable pay, commissions, contract work.
- **One earner** instead of two.
- **Dependents.**
- **Healthcare exposure.** Paying your own premium before Medicare (age 65).
- **Heavy fixed costs.** Big mortgage or high fixed bills.
- **Job replaceability.** Niche role means a longer job search.

Each factor makes your income less reliable or your bills harder to shrink. Together they estimate how long you might go without a paycheck. The reserve has to outlast that number.

### Working the couple's reserve

Start at 6 months.

- Two W-2 incomes, both stable: -2 months, down to 4.
- Two kids: +1 month, up to 5.
- $280,000 mortgage: +1 month, up to 6.
- Health insurance through employer: neutral (but on the list of what breaks if a job goes away).

Land on 6 months. $5,000 × 6 = $30,000 reserve target. They already hold $30,000 in cash, so they're fully funded. Now their job is to fence it off.

### When the reserve changes jobs

At retirement, the reserve does something different.

- While working, it covers months of **essentials**, replacing a lost paycheck.
- In retirement, there's no paycheck to replace. The reserve covers months of the **gap**: your spending minus your reliable income (Social Security, pension), so a bear market doesn't force a Bitcoin sale to cover it.

Run the couple at 67:

- Gap: $80,000 spending - $51,600 Social Security = $28,400/yr, or $2,400/mo.
- Retirement baseline: 18 months.
- 18 × $2,400 = $43,000 reserve target.

Working reserve: $30k. Retirement reserve: $43k. About 40% bigger on the same spending, because the reserve is doing a different job.

After several years of retirement, the risk fades: research shows sequence-of-returns risk — the risk of your portfolio dropping right after you retire — starts falling once you're about five years in. Past that point, the reserve can shrink toward a 12-month floor, or stay as high as three years if holding more cash is what lets you sleep. Early retirement is where a bad market can break the plan.

### The four-options question

Before you pick your months, answer this honestly:

*If Bitcoin dropped 50% and your income stopped for six months, what would you rather do?*

- **Hold more cash** now?
- **Sell some Bitcoin** then?
- **Borrow** against it?
- **Cut** spending hard?

Your answer is what your reserve size should reflect. All four options pay for the same six months, at different prices. The only one you can buy in advance is cash.

### Homework

Pick your target months. Multiply by your floor. Enter both into the reserve settings in the app.

### Known costs aren't emergencies

A **known cost** is anything on the calendar with a date on it:

- Kids' college tuition, four years starting in eight
- Roof replacement, in a ballpark year
- A car you'll buy in five years
- A wedding, a big trip, a home renovation
- Estimated taxes on a planned Roth conversion

Your reserve isn't for these. Reserves are for **unplanned** events. Known costs are on the calendar. Confuse them and you either drain the reserve for planned costs, or sell Bitcoin at the worst moment because you didn't pre-fund.

Every known cost gets its own **lane**: where those dollars sit between now and the due date.

### Match the lane to the timing

The lane depends on how far away the cost is. The further out, the more volatility the lane can tolerate.

| Timeframe | Where the money sits | What NOT to hold |
|---|---|---|
| 0 to 1 year | High-yield savings, T-bills, CDs | Anything volatile. Treat like part of the reserve. |
| 1 to 3 years | T-bills, short-term Treasuries, HYSA | Stocks, Bitcoin |
| 3 to 10 years | Balanced mix of stocks and bonds, I-Bond ladders | Bitcoin |
| 10+ years | A planned Bitcoin sell schedule can start here | Cash (drag is too high) |

For 10+ year costs, plan the sales into low-bracket years (Module 5).

### Pre-funding vs paying from flow

- **Small, dated costs** (a $2,000 vacation next summer) can be paid from flow. Set aside $200/mo starting six months before. No separate lane needed.
- **Big dated costs** (a $100,000 college bill in eight years) need their own pre-funded lane. Build $100k across eight years of small contributions instead of taking it as a Bitcoin sale in one year.

The threshold isn't a dollar number. If paying the cost all at once out of your monthly flow would break your surplus, pre-fund. If not, pay from flow.

### The college example

The couple's college bill is $100,000, starting in about 8 years and running four years.

Three options:

| Option | Outcome |
|---|---|
| **Pay from flow.** | $25k/yr tuition eats $25k of their $48k annual surplus. Doable, but it takes more than half of every dollar the waterfall was routing, for four straight years. |
| **Pre-fund from flow.** | Route ~$1,000/mo into an intermediate lane for 8 years. That's ~$96k contributed, or ~$110k with growth. Tuition years become a routing move from the lane, not a hit to surplus. |
| **Sell from Bitcoin.** | Plan sales into low-bracket years. Efficient if the tuition years line up with the bridge years, otherwise it forces realized gains in high-bracket working years. |

Which option is right depends on the numbers. But the plan needs to know the cost is coming and needs a lane for it.

### In the app

### What good looks like

- **This-month verdict** shows a positive surplus your household could actually move every month without strain.
- **Income map** lists every stream separately. Tax-free items are in Tax-free income, not folded into salary.
- **Living row** is your post-Keep/Cut/Reduce number, and it's visibly not your paycheck. If Living roughly equals Income, something went in the wrong row.
- **Verify Spending** shows reality within shouting distance of the Living number across two consecutive months. One-timers are excluded, not deleted. They stay under Ignored / one-time.
- **Reserve fields** have basis on Bare-bones, the real floor number in the field, and no warning strip.
- **Routing step 1** shows a real $X of $Y with full in N mo. If there's no end date, the build cap is $0.
- **Shield** is on the specific holding that is your emergency fund, not the whole account.
- **Waterfall** puts reserve first while short. Nothing gets routed to contributions ahead of the safety layer.
