# Orange Plan Academy — Advanced Library pre-dictation guide

Every Advanced lesson is optional. The learner completes the owning core planning area first, then watches the Advanced lesson only when the gate applies.

This file is the cleaned working surface for dictation. The protected scripts retain the detailed research, current-law qualifications, and app references. Do not try to read those long research drafts verbatim.

## Advanced lesson rule

- Open by saying who the lesson applies to or what decision triggered it.
- Explain the mechanism before giving the recommendation.
- Keep the strongest worked example and remove extra payoff lines.
- Name what would make the strategy fail.
- End with the comparison or rule the learner should carry back to the core plan.
- App clicks, current thresholds, and exact provider procedures belong in the reference or walkthrough unless they are essential to understanding the mechanism.

## A1.1 · How Orange Plan models Bitcoin: fat tails, correlations, floors, and caps

### Start from

> This lesson is for anyone who wants to understand what is happening underneath the confidence number and why Orange Plan does not model Bitcoin like a normal stock or bond fund.

### Cover it in this order

1. Most simple models use a normal bell curve. That makes extreme positive and negative years look far rarer than they have been in Bitcoin's actual history.
2. Explain fat tails in plain language: unusually large gains and losses continue to show up more often than a bell curve would predict.
3. Orange Plan uses a fat-tailed Bitcoin return model so a major down year is not treated like something that practically cannot happen.
4. Each asset has its own volatility and the volatility can decline as the asset matures. Do not freeze an exact current calibration into the spoken video unless it has just been verified.
5. The assets are not modeled as completely independent. Bitcoin, stocks, inflation, and other variables can move together or against each other in the simulated paths.
6. Floors and caps prevent the mathematical distribution from generating returns that are not useful or defensible for the model.
7. None of this proves the model will predict the future. It makes the confidence result more honest about the range of futures the plan may face.

### Avoid

- "You shouldn't have to take a number on faith" as a polished opening line.
- Quoting calibration figures that can become stale when the student text or app can carry them.
- Saying fat tails make the model correct.

### Finish with

Ask the learner whether each assumption is a number they could defend from evidence rather than a number chosen to make the plan look better.

## A3.1 · Borrow against Bitcoin without getting liquidated

### Start from

> This lesson is for anyone seriously considering a Bitcoin-backed loan. Before we talk about whether it belongs in the plan, I want to make sure the full mechanism and the failure points are clear.

### Cover it in this order

1. Explain the basic transaction: Bitcoin is pledged as collateral, the lender provides cash, interest is paid, and the collateral is returned when the loan is repaid under the agreement.
2. The attraction is access to cash without a voluntary Bitcoin sale. Loan proceeds are generally not income when there is a real repayment obligation, but a later collateral sale, liquidation, or debt cancellation can create tax consequences.
3. The central number is LTV: loan balance divided by collateral value. The borrower controls the starting balance and collateral posted; Bitcoin controls how quickly the denominator changes.
4. Show why the ratio accelerates. A 25% starting LTV becomes 50% after a 50% collateral decline and roughly 83% after a 70% decline.
5. Explain the provider's three lines: margin-call threshold, liquidation threshold, and any collateral-release threshold. The exact values and notice periods come from the actual agreement.
6. Size the cushion using the liquidation formula. Use the low-LTV worked examples to show that 10% and 20% starting LTVs survive very different drawdowns. Do not turn the 10% to 20% range into a guarantee or a universal recommendation.
7. Run the couple's $35,000 loan against 1.75 BTC at the illustrative $100,000 price. Show how LTV changes at $70,000, $50,000, $30,000, and $25,000 Bitcoin.
8. Decide the margin-call response before borrowing:
   - Post more collateral.
   - Pay down principal with cash.
   - Accept liquidation.
   Two of the three require unpledged resources.
9. Explain partial versus full liquidation and why the actual lender policy changes the downside.
10. Compare provider structure: custodial, collaborative, rehypothecation terms, who holds keys, withdrawal or release rules, fees, notice periods, and provider-independent recovery.
11. Write operating rules before the loan exists: purpose, maximum starting LTV, top-up and paydown lines, no-go conditions, responsible person, and the response to a 50% to 80% decline.
12. In the app, use the lender's actual thresholds rather than the defaults and run the drawdown scenario.

### Avoid

- "A 40% drop is an ordinary Tuesday" or other quotable exaggerations.
- Saying the customer legally owns collateral in exactly the same way under every lender structure.
- Saying collaborative custody automatically eliminates provider risk.
- Saying a loan is tax-free.

### Finish with

The decision is whether the household should borrow at all, the starting LTV it can support, the provider structure it accepts, and the resources available for a margin call. A household that cannot fund a margin call cannot treat the loan as safely manageable.

## A3.2 · The four ways debt can build wealth

### Start from

> The core debt lesson set the limits. This lesson covers the situations where debt may be used on purpose instead of treated as something that always has to be eliminated.

### Cover it in this order

1. **The spread.** Borrow at a low fixed cost while capital remains invested in something expected to earn more. The failure is that the return is uncertain while the payment is not.
2. Use Austin's mortgage example if he wants it. Keep the point simple: he used a smaller down payment and kept more capital in Bitcoin because the mortgage was affordable and the reserve was solid.
3. **Borrow instead of selling.** This can delay a sale and preserve exposure, but adds interest, collateral, repayment, counterparty, and liquidation risk. It creates a choice about when to sell; it does not create a permanent no-sale strategy.
4. **Strategic Bitcoin-backed borrowing.** Explain why adding leverage to buy more Bitcoin requires a much higher standard than simply retaining existing low-rate debt. Concentration limits and written operating rules matter.
5. **Keep low-rate debt deliberately.** A comfortable low-rate mortgage may preserve flexibility and capital. A credit line established while income is strong may create future optionality, but an unused line can still tempt bad decisions and variable rates can move.
6. Preview the retirement variation: a household may compare selling, borrowing, or blended strategies, but the complete decision is made in the retirement-income material.
7. Every play is a comparison against the defensive limits. If it violates the debt ceiling, reserve, or cash-flow capacity, the upside story does not rescue it.

### Avoid

- Calling debt a power tool that can "take your hand off."
- Presenting expected investment return minus debt rate as guaranteed earned spread.
- Saying none is a "common answer" just to reassure the learner.

### Finish with

The learner chooses one play or none, names the failure mode before the upside, and writes the operating rules before acting.

## A4.1 · The price-context check before a large move

### Start from

> Use this short check before a large Bitcoin buy, sale, or loan, especially when the last few months of price action are creating urgency.

### Cover it in this order

1. Look at the recent 3-, 6-, 9-, and 12-month window. This is mostly useful for naming the emotion: FOMO after a sharp rise or fear after a sharp decline.
2. Then look at the 2- to 5-year window to place the recent move in a longer context.
3. Describe the proposed decision without using the recent price move as the reason.
4. Tie it to an existing rule: target allocation, reserve, debt ceiling, tax plan, retirement-income strategy, or another specific job.
5. Ask whether the move would still make sense if the next month's price went the other direction.
6. If price is doing most of the work, pause and model the decision against the plan. That does not automatically make it wrong; it means the plan has not yet justified it.

### Avoid

- "Name what is in the room."
- "The recent window names the emotion; the long window gives it context."
- "Is the plan talking or the price talking?" as a slogan repeated several times.

### Finish with

The learner can explain the move from the plan without relying on the fact that Bitcoin has recently gone up or down.

## A5.1 · RMD risk and Roth conversions

### Start from

> This lesson is for anyone whose traditional retirement accounts may become large enough to create forced taxable income later, especially when there are lower-income years available before required distributions begin.

### Cover it in this order

1. Define an RMD and explain the general calculation: prior year-end balance divided by the applicable life-expectancy factor.
2. RMD starting age depends on birth cohort. For the course couple, current law points to age 75. Do not teach one age as universal.
3. Traditional money is not inherently bad. The problem is a mismatch between the deduction received while contributing and the tax rate or forced income created later.
4. Use the 7% contribution-growth illustration to show how a traditional account may reach about $1.14 million by age 75 and create an illustrative first RMD around $46,000 under the stated factor. Keep the figures labeled as illustrations.
5. Use the flat 20% return case only as an extreme sensitivity example showing why fast growth inside a traditional account deserves attention. Do not present it as a baseline forecast.
6. Explain a Roth conversion: ordinary income is recognized in the conversion year and the converted amount moves to Roth, where future qualified distributions can be tax-free and the original owner has no lifetime RMD under current law.
7. The RMD due for a year cannot itself be converted. It must be distributed first; a separate conversion may follow.
8. A lower Bitcoin price can allow the same dollar conversion to move more units, but that does not make market timing the entire decision.
9. Do not stop at the federal bracket. Include Marketplace credits, Social Security taxation, Medicare IRMAA, NIIT, capital-gain room, state tax, deductions, and credits.
10. Paying tax from outside funds may keep more money in Roth, but reserve needs and liquidity come first.
11. Inherited-account rules are more complicated than "every child has ten years." Eligible designated beneficiaries and annual-distribution rules can differ.

### Finish with

The learner identifies the low-income years worth reviewing, models several conversion sizes including zero, and brings an all-in cost range to the tax professional.

## A5.2 · Harvesting losses and gains

### Start from

> This lesson covers two tax moves that go in opposite directions: realizing a loss when the loss is valuable, and realizing a gain when the tax cost of raising basis is low enough.

### Cover it in this order

1. A capital loss offsets capital gains first. Remaining net losses may have a limited current deduction and carry forward. The value depends on what the loss eventually offsets.
2. The lot must actually be identified under the current self-custody or broker rules. HIFO in the app is not enough if the required real-time records or broker instruction do not exist.
3. Under current federal law, the stock wash-sale rule generally does not apply to spot Bitcoin because it is treated as property for that rule. Keep this statement narrow and current-year dependent.
4. Price the whole loss-harvest round trip: fees, spread, network cost, price movement, recordkeeping risk, new holding period, and the larger future gain created by the lower replacement basis.
5. Gain harvesting intentionally realizes a long-term gain during a low all-in tax year and may reset basis higher.
6. A federal 0% long-term-gain band does not mean zero total cost. Ordinary income fills the stack first, and state tax, Marketplace credits, NIIT, and other thresholds may move.
7. Use the $100,000 value and $40,000 basis example. If only part of the $60,000 gain fits at an acceptable all-in cost, harvest only that part or do nothing.
8. HIFO can reduce current gain but leaves lower-basis units for later. The objective is a lifetime tax path, not winning the current year's tax bill.

### Finish with

The learner models one loss harvest and one gain harvest, includes execution and future-basis costs, and keeps the move as a possibility until current-year treatment is confirmed.

## A5.3 · State taxes and relocation

### Start from

> This lesson is for anyone seriously considering a move or a large taxable sale where the state-tax difference could be meaningful.

### Cover it in this order

1. State treatment of capital gains varies and can create a large difference on a large Bitcoin sale. Use the app to convert the difference into dollars rather than relying on a map or headline rate.
2. Do not freeze current state rates into the video.
3. Residency is not determined only by the address on the sale date. States may consider domicile, days present, homes, family, business ties, part-year rules, source income, community-property rules, trusts, and ownership structure.
4. Changing an address does not automatically establish a new domicile or end the former state's claims.
5. Use the right order: decide whether the move fits the life the household wants, learn the legal residency requirements, make and document the real move, and then model the transaction under a defensible assumption.
6. Add the rest of the move: housing, property tax, insurance, healthcare, travel, local taxes, legal costs, and the value of the life being left.
7. The result may be that the move is already part of the life plan, is a real option needing professional analysis, or is not on the table and the state tax should simply remain in the plan.

### Avoid

- "Price the state-tax difference without pretending a map can decide where you live."
- A three-answer payoff construction at the end.

### Finish with

State tax becomes one number inside a real lifestyle decision, not the only reason for the move.

## A6.1 · Health insurance between retiring and Medicare

### Start from

> This lesson is for anyone planning to stop working before Medicare eligibility and needing to price the years without employer coverage.

### Cover it in this order

1. Treat the healthcare bridge as a dated expense from retirement until Medicare eligibility. Include premiums, expected out-of-pocket costs, and a stress allowance.
2. Employer continuation such as COBRA may preserve the same plan for a limited period, often at the full premium plus an administrative charge. Use the actual notice rather than one universal duration.
3. Marketplace pricing depends on household income, household size, benchmark plan, location, and current law. Do not reuse old subsidy cliffs or premium examples.
4. MAGI is part of the healthcare cost. Roth conversions, gains, business income, and retirement distributions can change premium credits. The tax and healthcare assumptions must agree.
5. Health-sharing can be discussed through Austin's experience, but it is not health insurance and generally does not create the same legal payment obligation. Read the exact current member terms.
6. A spouse's employer plan, retiree plan, union plan, or another eligibility path may be the best option. Compare the incremental family cost, deductible, network, and employer contribution.
7. Compare each real option on premium, deductible, out-of-pocket maximum, network, prescriptions, routine cost, stress cost, tax assumptions, duration, and what payment is legally guaranteed.
8. Medicare changes the bridge at 65, but enrollment timing, HSA rules, Medigap, Medicare Advantage, prescription coverage, and IRMAA still require advance review.
9. Enter the best current estimate as a dated expense and run a higher-cost scenario.

### Finish with

The bridge has a current source, start and end dates, a stress case, and a scheduled next enrollment or Medicare review.

## A6.2 · Sell, borrow, or hold: funding a year of spending

### Start from

> This lesson is for a Bitcoin-heavy household close enough to retirement that it is deciding how next year's spending will actually be funded.

### Cover it in this order

1. Use the same annual spending need and the same time horizon for all three paths.
2. **Sell:** no loan or lender risk, but the household gives up the units sold and may realize a taxable gain based on the identified lots. A 0% federal gain rate does not automatically mean zero total cost.
3. **Borrow:** loan proceeds are generally not income when there is a real repayment obligation, but interest, fees, liquidation, collateral sales, cancellation, counterparty terms, repayment source, and estate obligations remain.
4. **Hold and use Reserve or Bridge:** preserves Bitcoin and adds no loan, but consumes liquidity that already has another job and may create a later forced sale if the refill policy fails.
5. Under current federal law, inherited taxable property generally receives a date-of-death basis rule, but gifts, trusts, estate inclusion, reporting rules, and future law can change the outcome. Do not say every embedded gain simply disappears.
6. Do not compare a one-time tax rate directly with an annual interest rate. Compare dollars over the same period, including future Bitcoin exposure and drawdown risk.
7. Extend each path to five years:
   - Sell: units, basis, tax, Bitcoin remaining.
   - Borrow: balance, interest, collateral, LTV, top-ups, repayment, and drawdown outcome.
   - Hold: reserve and Bridge depletion, refills, and later-sale risk.
8. Most households may use different paths in different years. Do not force one strategy into a forever rule.
9. Apply the family test before borrowing. The spouse or heir should be able to explain who holds collateral, liquidation lines, repayment source, lender failure, and the estate's responsibility.

### Finish with

The choice is supported by dollars over the same horizon and can be explained without relying on the belief that Bitcoin will simply rise before the risk matters.

## A7.1 · Advanced custody: passphrase, multisig, and collaborative custody

### Start from

> This lesson is for households that have already completed the core custody plan and are considering more separation than a single-signature hardware wallet provides.

### Cover it in this order

1. Start with two tests: can one item or person spend alone, and can one loss permanently block recovery?
2. Explain a BIP39 passphrase as an optional string that derives a different wallet from a compatible mnemonic. A typo creates a valid but different wallet rather than a simple error.
3. What it buys: the mnemonic alone does not reveal the intended passphrase wallet when the elements are separated. What it costs: losing either may make the wallet unrecoverable.
4. A passphrase is not multisig. There are not two independent signers or an on-chain threshold.
5. Austin's seven-random-word approach is an operational recommendation, not a protocol minimum. The passphrase needs independent generation, exact offline backups, separation, and a tested recovery path.
6. Explain 2-of-3 multisig: any two keys authorize a spend, one key cannot, and one key may be lost.
7. The recovery package also needs the policy or descriptor and enough script, derivation, and origin information for compatible software. The descriptor can reveal wallet information but cannot sign.
8. Key distribution should use independent failure domains. The exact people and locations are threat-model and estate decisions.
9. Collaborative custody may provide setup, review, recovery assistance, or a key. Provider independence must be proven: the client controls enough keys, has the policy, knows compatible software, and can recover and sign outside the provider's normal interface.
10. Choose passphrase when the household can protect two exact elements and wants less hardware complexity. Choose multisig when threshold signing and loss tolerance justify the operational load. Choose collaborative custody only when the assistance is valuable and provider independence has been verified.
11. Test the exact setup, including every threshold combination the family depends on and the legal roles around the keys.

### Finish with

The added complexity removes a named failure, survives the failure it was designed for, and can be recovered by the family without guessing or depending on one vendor.

## A7.2 · What self-custody actually asks of you

### Start from

> A client described self-custody in a way that I think is useful: when you take custody, you become both a possible point of failure and a possible attack vector.

### Cover it in this order

1. Most of life outsources difficult security jobs to institutions. Bitcoin gives the owner the choice to take this particular job back.
2. The job is not simply buying a hardware wallet. It includes protecting recovery material, testing recovery, maintaining devices and software, noticing new failure points, and making sure the family process still works.
3. There are three honest answers:
   - Take the whole job.
   - Keep part of the job and use collaborative or institutional help for the rest.
   - Decide not to take it yet.
4. Taking responsibility the household will not maintain is not automatically safer or more sovereign.
5. Match the setup to the amount, skill, family, and willingness to maintain the process.

### Avoid

- Turning "whole job, part of it, none" into a dramatic three-part payoff.
- Ending with an identity-versus-responsibility slogan.

### Finish with

The learner writes which responsibilities they will own, which ones they want help carrying, and the recovery task that must be proven before moving more Bitcoin.

## A7.3 · Concentration: one institution, one vendor, one firmware

### Start from

> This lesson is for anyone whose custody setup looks diversified on the surface but can still be reached by one institution, one login, one device family, or one recovery process.

### Cover it in this order

1. Custody type and concentration are separate questions. Strong institutional custody can still place every liquid asset behind one provider. Self-custody can still put every satoshi behind one device model, firmware family, wallet implementation, and recovery process.
2. Institution concentration matters when losing access for months would change the plan, the account funds near-term spending, or one provider holds every taxable, retirement, or lending relationship.
3. A second institution only helps when it genuinely fails differently. Shared email, phone, bank, identity provider, or underlying custodian can preserve the same concentration.
4. Every extra account creates maintenance: authentication, tax records, beneficiary forms, recovery, and executor instructions. Several weak accounts may be worse than one hardened account.
5. A second hardware vendor or implementation may reduce correlated vendor or firmware risk, but an unfamiliar setup adds human and recovery risk.
6. Diversification works only across independent failure domains: different vendors, implementations, authentication paths, locations, or part institutional and part self-custody.
7. Add complexity only when one flaw or event can still reach everything and the household can maintain the second path.

### Finish with

The remaining concentration is visible and deliberate, and one failure cannot remove every asset the household needs for the plan.

## A7.4 · Wallet operations: UTXOs, dust, consolidation, and addresses

### Start from

> This lesson is for anyone whose hardware wallet and recovery process already work and who now needs to understand the operational choices inside the wallet.

### Cover it in this order

1. A Bitcoin wallet tracks unspent transaction outputs rather than one protocol-level account balance. Incoming transactions create outputs; later spends use outputs as inputs and usually create change.
2. Many small inputs can make a later transaction larger and more expensive. Do not call every small output dust. Technical dust and an output that is merely uneconomical at a high fee rate are different.
3. Austin's 0.01 to 0.02 BTC exchange-transfer threshold is a rule of thumb, not a protocol rule. Compare current fees, exchange exposure, withdrawal fees, and future spend cost. Move sooner when counterparty exposure becomes too large.
4. Consolidation combines several outputs into a new wallet-controlled output. It may reduce future input count when fees are low.
5. Consolidation also links activity, costs a fee now, and can create a larger privacy-visible cluster. It is a fee-versus-privacy decision, not automatic cleanup.
6. Use fresh receive addresses when the wallet provides them and verify meaningful addresses on the trusted display.
7. Descriptors and extended public keys cannot sign by themselves but can reveal addresses and history, so they belong in controlled recovery material rather than public notes.
8. Labels and coin control can support tax and privacy decisions, but selecting the wrong output can break the intended tax identification, combine private clusters, or create inefficient change.

### Finish with

The transfer threshold is tied to current fees and counterparty exposure, and any consolidation decision deliberately accepts the privacy trade-off.

## A8.1 · Do you need a trust, and which kind of problem would it solve?

### Start from

> This lesson is for a household whose core estate review found a real complexity problem that may deserve a trust conversation. We are not going to choose a trust from one net-worth number or the fact that the household owns Bitcoin.

### Cover it in this order

1. A trust is a legal and fiduciary relationship governed by the actual document and state law. Ownership, control, tax treatment, creditor rights, and trustee duties do not come from the label alone.
2. The core triggers create attorney questions: minor or vulnerable beneficiaries, blended family, business ownership, property in several states, incapacity, privacy or probate concerns, a custody process another person must operate, possible estate-tax exposure, or a concentrated asset a fiduciary may retain.
3. One trigger does not mechanically require a trust.
4. A revocable living trust may help with incapacity management and avoid probate for assets that are actually titled or assigned to it. Signing an unfunded trust does not move assets or automatically save estate tax.
5. An irrevocable trust can change ownership, control, taxation, creditor exposure, estate inclusion, and basis, but the result depends on funding, retained powers, beneficiaries, grantor-trust status, governing state, and actual terms.
6. Separate a succession problem from an estate-tax problem. Minor children or complex custody may create a succession problem at modest wealth; estate-tax exposure depends on current law, ownership, deductions, prior gifts, and future value.
7. The legal owner and the key plan must agree. A trust that owns Bitcoin but cannot practically execute an authorized transaction is incomplete. One trustee holding enough signing material may recreate a single-person risk.
8. Most states use some prudent-investor framework. A trustee expected to retain concentrated Bitcoin needs state-specific authority and protection. There is no universal "Bitcoin waiver" sentence.
9. The attorney may consider express retention authority, modified diversification duties, directed-trust structures, special trustees, trust protectors, investment advisers, or other tools allowed by the governing law.
10. Estate-tax planning and basis planning can pull in different directions. The attorney and CPA need to model ownership, estate inclusion, and basis together.
11. For the course couple, minor children and concentrated Bitcoin justify the conversation but do not prove whether a revocable trust, irrevocable trust, or attorney-supervised will plan is best.
12. "No trust currently indicated" is a valid dated result after the review.

### Avoid

- "The gate is a conversation trigger" as a heading.
- A generic list of reasons not to choose a trust as a dramatic closing.
- Saying every inherited asset receives a step-up or every irrevocable trust removes property from the estate.

### Finish with

The household can name the actual problem a trust would solve, knows which assets already pass outside probate, and brings the ownership, basis, fiduciary, and custody questions to a state-licensed attorney and CPA.
