# Slide Deck Authority

**Austin decision — 2026-08-26**

The original Orange Plan slide decks are the structural source of truth for the required core course. They define the order in which a learner understands the problem, makes the decision, and implements it.

This corrects the earlier rebuild instruction that treated the decks as loose concept material rather than course flow. The decks may be adapted to the current app and to a cleaner self-paced course, but their decision sequence may not be discarded in favor of generic lesson templates or page-by-page app navigation.

## Source hierarchy

Use these sources in this order:

1. **Austin's dictation** — authority for the spoken explanation, examples, recommendations, and judgment calls.
2. **Austin's slide decks** — authority for module flow, teaching sequence, primary decisions, and the visual logic of the course.
3. **Current Orange Plan production app** — authority for what exists, what is calculated, where data is entered, and the exact walkthrough mechanics.
4. **Primary-source research** — verifies facts, law, math, and technical claims. It does not invent the lesson's philosophy or structure.
5. **Existing generated scripts** — disposable drafts. They have no authority when they conflict with the four sources above.

## How to adapt a deck

“Deck authority” does not mean reading old slides word for word or preserving outdated clicks.

- Keep the **decision flow** and the reason each step follows the prior one.
- Update app labels, fields, and walkthrough actions against current production.
- Split or combine lessons only when it makes the self-paced teaching clearer without changing the underlying sequence.
- Remove repeated copywriting structures, generic summaries, and invented planning rules.
- Put current figures and changing legal details in student text or the app, not in evergreen spoken video.

## Teach lesson versus walkthrough

A teach lesson explains:

- what the concept means;
- why it affects the plan;
- the decision the learner has to make;
- the information or trade-offs needed to make it.

A walkthrough performs the implementation:

- where to click;
- what to enter;
- what the app calculates;
- what to save;
- how to verify the result.

A teach lesson should not repeat the click path. When a walkthrough follows, the final transition should sound natural, for example:

> In the walkthrough below this lesson, I'll show you exactly where to enter your bare-bones spending, choose the number of reserve months, and set the funding plan in Orange Plan.

Do not say that the learner must calculate something manually unless the current app actually requires it.

## Core module flow

### Module 1 — Foundation

Preserve the Foundation deck's sequence while adapting it to short onboarding and Build Your Plan:

1. understand what onboarding estimated;
2. gather and verify the real baseline;
3. add the real accounts and current holdings in Foundation;
4. understand baseline facts, assumptions, expected life events, and scenarios;
5. choose the assumptions for the first working model;
6. understand that the finished confidence-based retirement date comes after the full plan is built;
7. update the plan when real inputs change, not whenever the Bitcoin price moves.

Income, spending, debts, tax history, and retirement-income strategy are entered in the modules that own them.

### Module 2 — Cash flow, reserve, and expected life events

Follow the Cash Flow deck:

1. map every income stream and its reliability;
2. understand real spending and separate normal from bare-bones;
3. use Keep / Cut / Reduce to improve usable surplus;
4. choose the monthly surplus the household can reliably move;
5. size the emergency reserve and identify the gap;
6. choose the funding pace and real-world automation;
7. identify expected life events that materially change future income, spending, or assets;
8. add those life events to the plan and understand how they change future cash flow.

This module teaches future-cost awareness and how life events work. It should not become an invented universal investment-lane doctrine. Financing may be named as one possible funding source, but debt strategy belongs in Module 3.

The lesson should explain that surplus eventually funds investments and other priorities. The detailed account and investment routing belongs in Module 4.

### Optional college lesson

The college lesson must answer the decisions Austin identified:

1. How much are the parents actually committing to help with?
2. What might college realistically cost for that commitment?
3. How will the commitment be paid for — Bitcoin, a 529, current cash flow while enrolled, other investments, student work, aid, or a bounded amount of student loans?
4. How much, if anything, needs to be saved each month?
5. What changes as enrollment gets closer?

Do not assume a 529 is the default. For a Bitcoiner with more than five years, Bitcoin may remain the primary savings asset. The lesson should explain the trade-offs and the app's actual capabilities before presenting a formula or recommendation.

The possible “one-third current cash flow or investments, one-third saved in advance, one-third student loans” approach is a research and dictation prompt, not an approved course rule.

### Module 3 — Debt strategy

Follow the Debt deck's decision sequence:

1. review every debt, balance, rate, payment, and variable-rate or collateral risk;
2. understand when debt creates liquidity, spread, or tax flexibility and when it creates fragility;
3. establish the household's actual tolerance for carrying debt;
4. use debt-to-assets and debt-to-income as context, not as automatic commands;
5. decide the job and action for every debt;
6. understand how Bitcoin volatility changes leverage risk;
7. keep Bitcoin-backed borrowing in the advanced path unless it is necessary to explain a current loan;
8. feed the chosen extra-payment amount into the contribution waterfall.

### Module 4 — Contributions, accounts, and allocation

Use the Allocation deck's order. This module owns the missing “what happens to the surplus?” instruction:

1. review current accounts, holdings, contribution destinations, and obvious mismatches;
2. choose the Bitcoin allocation the learner understands and can hold through a major drawdown;
3. decide what the next dollar is for: Reserve, Bridge, or Legacy / Forever;
4. set the target allocation for those jobs;
5. stress-test the dollar loss, not only the percentage;
6. build the contribution waterfall:
   - Reserve, when still needed;
   - employer match, when available;
   - extra debt payments selected in Module 3;
   - taxable versus retirement based on Bridge versus long-term needs;
   - target allocation inside the chosen account;
7. choose Roth versus Traditional based on when the tax is paid;
8. confirm that the chosen account can actually hold the intended Bitcoin exposure;
9. update the contribution plan and the real-world elections, transfers, and automations.

The Module 4 walkthrough must visibly show how the learner directs investable surplus, saves the planned contribution amounts, assigns account timeframes, sets the target mix, and verifies what routes now versus what begins after Reserve is funded.

### Module 5 — Tax strategy

Follow the Tax deck while keeping changing law out of evergreen narration:

1. establish cost-basis quality;
2. understand taxable, tax-deferred, and Roth money;
3. identify the lifetime tax window;
4. recognize future RMD pressure;
5. compare Roth conversions, gain/loss harvesting, and state-tax decisions;
6. choose one or two moves worth modeling now and identify what should wait;
7. implement only after the current-year facts are verified.

### Module 6 — Retirement income

Follow the Retirement Income deck:

1. confirm retirement spending;
2. build the durable income floor;
3. calculate the gap the portfolio must cover;
4. map cash flow year by year;
5. identify and fund the Bridge years;
6. choose the withdrawal order and income waterfall;
7. decide how Reserve, Bridge, and Legacy refill each other;
8. compare selling, borrowing, and holding where applicable;
9. set the annual spending guardrails;
10. set the reserve-refill rule.

### Module 7 — Custody

Follow the Custody deck:

1. separate operational custody from legal inheritance;
2. choose a custody level appropriate to the amount and household;
3. set up and test recovery using the exact device process;
4. identify the largest single points of failure;
5. harden accounts and establish scam rules;
6. compare passphrase, collaborative custody, and DIY multisig only when added complexity solves a real risk;
7. document the process without recording secrets.

### Module 8 — Estate and inheritance

Follow the Estate deck, corrected by the legal and custody research:

1. choose the person responsible for carrying out the plan;
2. identify the core legal documents and beneficiary work;
3. align legal authority with the technical custody process without inventing one universal seed/passphrase/key split;
4. create a no-secrets heir letter and executor process;
5. establish a tested delivery or dead-man-switch backstop;
6. treat trusts as an advanced gate, not a default recommendation.

### Module 9 — Run and maintain the plan

This module completes the loop:

1. confirm every Build Your Plan area is intentionally complete;
2. run the first full 1,000-path confidence check;
3. choose the confidence target and read the earliest retirement date;
4. test decisions in Scenarios without corrupting the baseline;
5. establish monthly, annual, and trigger-event review rhythms;
6. update when life changes, not because market noise creates urgency.

## Pre-dictation status rule

A non-dictated script is a **pre-dictation draft**, not an Austin script and not cleared for filming merely because automated checks pass.

Before Austin dictates a lesson, the draft must pass three checks:

1. Its flow follows the applicable slide deck.
2. Its app claims match current production.
3. It explains a useful decision without filler copywriting or duplicate walkthrough instructions.

Austin's dictation then becomes the spoken authority.
