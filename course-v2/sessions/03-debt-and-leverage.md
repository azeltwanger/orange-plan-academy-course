# Session 3 — Build the debt and leverage plan

**Status:** PRE-DICTATION FILMING DRAFT  
**Audience:** Bitcoin households with current debt, available credit, or interest in using debt strategically  
**Source basis:** Austin's debt deck and detailed breakdown, prior course material, de-identified coaching patterns, the fictional Reed household, and the current Orange Plan debt / leverage architecture  
**Recording boundary:** durable concepts may be dictated after acceptance; exact app labels and routes wait for the approved redesign surface

## What the member builds

By the end of this session, the member will have:

- every debt and credit facility entered with current terms;
- a clear read on monthly payment pressure;
- current and stressed balance-sheet leverage;
- one job assigned to every debt;
- a separate decision on whether strategic borrowing belongs in the plan;
- household leverage rules written before a new opportunity appears;
- a final extra-debt amount returned to Cash Flow for the contribution waterfall.

## Why this session comes before Allocation

The monthly surplus has several possible jobs. Extra debt is one of them. The contribution plan cannot be called complete until the household decides whether any of the surplus belongs to debt payoff.

This session handles that decision first. Session 4 then routes the remaining dollars into the right accounts and holdings.

# 3.1 — Measure payment pressure before comparing returns

**Target runtime:** 5–7 minutes  
**Member decision:** How much required debt payment can this household carry without making the plan fragile?

## Opening

Debt starts with the monthly payment because the payment is what the household has to survive every month.

A balance can look reasonable on a net-worth statement while the payment is still squeezing cash flow. A low rate can be mathematically attractive while the payment leaves no room for the Reserve, new investment, or an unexpected expense. A higher rate can be manageable for a short period when the payment is small and the payoff plan is clear.

The first question is simple:

> Can the household make every required payment, keep the Reserve intact, and continue following the plan during a bad year?

## Debt tolerance

The math gives the household a range. Personal tolerance decides where inside that range the household is willing to live.

Some people can carry a fixed mortgage for decades without thinking about it. Other people feel every balance even when the plan shows plenty of room. Peace of mind has value. The plan should account for that without turning every debt decision into a rule that all debt must disappear.

Ask two different questions:

1. Does this debt bother me?
2. Does this debt make the plan fragile?

The first affects quality of life. The second affects the ability to keep paying bills, hold Bitcoin through a drawdown, and avoid a forced sale.

## Debt-to-income

Debt-to-income is the monthly-payment lens.

**Formula:**

> Required monthly debt payments ÷ gross monthly income

Use it to answer:

- How much of income is already committed before saving or investing?
- Would one income loss create a problem?
- Does a variable business income make the same payment burden harder to carry?
- How much room remains for the Reserve and the next-dollar plan?

The ratio is context, not a personal verdict. A household with two reliable incomes, a strong Reserve, and flexible spending can carry more payment than a household with one variable income and high fixed costs.

## Continuing household

Alex and Morgan have:

- a low-rate mortgage;
- a variable HELOC;
- an auto loan;
- a high-rate credit-card balance;
- a business equipment loan.

The card balance is relatively small compared with the home loan, but its rate and payment make it the clearest current problem. The mortgage is the largest balance, yet the fixed rate and manageable payment may make it the least urgent debt.

This is why debt is reviewed row by row rather than sorted only by balance.

## Member action

For every debt, write down:

- current balance;
- required payment;
- rate;
- fixed or variable;
- remaining term;
- what secures it;
- whether the payment would still be manageable after an income drop.

## Visual

A simple debt ledger with five columns:

| Debt | Required payment | Rate type | Collateral | Payment-pressure read |
|---|---:|---|---|---|
| Mortgage |  | Fixed | Home | Comfortable / tight |
| HELOC |  | Variable | Home | Comfortable / tight |
| Card |  | Variable | Unsecured | Comfortable / tight |
| Auto |  | Fixed | Vehicle | Comfortable / tight |
| Business loan |  | Fixed or variable | Business asset / guarantee | Comfortable / tight |

## Done when

- Every required payment is current.
- Variable rates are identified.
- The household can explain what would break first after an income drop.
- Debt tolerance and debt fragility have been considered separately.

# 3.2 — Measure leverage at today's price and after a Bitcoin drawdown

**Target runtime:** 6–8 minutes  
**Member decision:** What balance-sheet leverage can the household carry after the asset side falls?

## Opening

Debt-to-income changes when income or payments change. Debt-to-assets can change overnight because Bitcoin moves the asset side of the equation.

That makes leverage look safest after Bitcoin has risen and most dangerous after it has fallen. The household needs both views before setting a debt range.

## Debt-to-assets

**Formula:**

> Total debt ÷ total assets

This is the balance-sheet leverage lens. It answers:

- How much of the asset base is financed?
- How much equity is left after the debt?
- How dependent is the plan on current asset values?
- Would a major drawdown make the household feel trapped?

Do not stop at today's ratio.

## The stressed ratio

Run the same calculation after a major Bitcoin drawdown and, where relevant, lower values for other volatile assets.

The debt balance may barely move while the assets fall quickly. That can create three forms of pressure:

1. The household feels poorer and starts making emotional decisions.
2. A lender may require more collateral or reduce available credit.
3. Selling an asset to remove the debt may lock in the drawdown.

The stress test should use dollars as well as percentages. “Bitcoin down 70%” feels abstract. “Our net worth falls by $640,000 while every payment remains due” is a decision the household can understand.

## Secured debt and LTV

For debt tied to a specific asset, also calculate loan-to-value.

**Formula:**

> Loan balance ÷ current collateral value

The important number is the space between the current LTV and the lender's action thresholds.

For a Bitcoin-backed loan, falling Bitcoin raises LTV. For a HELOC or mortgage, falling home value raises the combined housing leverage. For a securities-backed line or margin loan, falling investments can reduce borrowing capacity or force action.

Terms vary by lender. The core course teaches the mechanism. The actual contract controls the thresholds, notices, remedies, and timing.

## Continuing household

Alex and Morgan look comfortable when Bitcoin and the home are valued at today's levels. The stress test changes the read:

- direct Bitcoin and the Bitcoin ETF fall sharply;
- the home is modeled at a lower value;
- the mortgage, HELOC, auto, card, and business-loan balances remain;
- the variable-rate payment is modeled higher.

The point is not to prove the household should have no leverage. The point is to find the amount they can still carry when the asset values are less favorable.

## Member action

Record three numbers:

1. Current debt-to-income.
2. Current debt-to-assets.
3. Stressed debt-to-assets after the chosen drawdown.

Then answer:

> Would this stressed balance sheet still let us make the payments and hold our long-term assets without panic or a forced sale?

## Visual

Two balance sheets side by side:

- **Today:** current Bitcoin, home, investments, cash, and debt.
- **Drawdown:** lower volatile-asset values, same debt, higher variable-rate payment where relevant.

Show the debt-to-assets ratio beneath both.

## Done when

- Current and stressed leverage are visible.
- Every collateralized debt has an LTV or equivalent collateral read.
- The household understands which debt becomes more dangerous when asset values fall.
- The household has a preliminary leverage range it could live with in both views.

# 3.3 — Give every current debt a job

**Target runtime:** 6–8 minutes  
**Member decision:** Keep, pay extra, refinance, consolidate, restructure, pay off, or monitor each debt?

## Opening

Every debt needs one job and one reason.

A generic “pay debt” goal is hard to implement because the debts have different costs, payment pressure, collateral, and strategic value. The plan needs a row-by-row decision.

## The seven jobs

### Keep the minimum

Use this when the payment is comfortable, the rate and terms are acceptable, and the capital has a more important job elsewhere.

A low fixed mortgage may belong here when paying it off would drain liquidity, create a taxable sale, or delay higher-priority funding.

### Pay extra

Use this when removing the debt is the strongest current use of part of the surplus.

High rates, high payment pressure, short payoff windows, or meaningful emotional relief can justify an extra amount.

### Refinance

Use this when the same balance may be carried on better terms. Compare the new rate, fees, term, collateral, total interest, and whether the lower payment simply extends the debt much longer.

### Consolidate

Use this when several obligations can become one manageable payment without creating a worse collateral or behavioral problem.

Moving unsecured debt onto the home can lower the rate while increasing the consequence of failure. The lower payment is only part of the decision.

### Restructure

Use this when the payment shape is the problem. Examples include changing amortization, converting a variable rate, negotiating business-debt terms, or replacing a balloon payment with a planned schedule.

### Pay off

Use this when the guaranteed cost, payment pressure, collateral risk, or stress is more damaging than the lost liquidity.

### Monitor

Use this when the current action is to keep the debt but the risk can change quickly. Variable-rate, margin, HELOC, and Bitcoin-backed debt often need explicit review triggers.

## Three decisions that stay separate

Do not collapse these into one question:

1. Should the household keep buying Bitcoin from monthly cash flow?
2. Should the household pay an existing debt faster?
3. Should the household add new debt to buy Bitcoin or another asset?

A household can keep a low-rate mortgage, pay off a high-rate card, continue a smaller Bitcoin purchase, and decline a new leveraged purchase. Each decision uses different facts.

## Continuing household

A reasonable draft for Alex and Morgan might look like this:

- Credit card: pay off quickly because the cost and payment are damaging.
- Mortgage: keep the minimum because the rate is fixed and liquidity has other jobs.
- HELOC: monitor or pay extra because the rate is variable and the home secures it.
- Auto loan: compare a moderate extra payment with Reserve and Bridge funding.
- Business equipment loan: keep or restructure based on whether the equipment is producing enough cash flow to carry the payment.

These are teaching decisions for the fictional case. The member uses the same comparison with their own numbers.

## Member action

Complete one sentence for each debt:

> We will [job] this debt because [reason]. The decision changes if [trigger].

Example:

> We will keep the mortgage minimum because the payment is comfortable and the Reserve and Bridge need the cash more. We will revisit if income falls or the payment becomes a larger share of cash flow.

## Done when

- Every debt has one selected job.
- Every job has a reason tied to cost, payment pressure, collateral, liquidity, or tolerance.
- Every monitored debt has a trigger for review.
- The monthly extra-payment total is known.

# 3.4 — Use debt strategically from a defensive foundation

**Target runtime:** 8–10 minutes  
**Member decision:** Does strategic borrowing belong in this plan, and for what purpose?

## Opening

Debt can preserve liquidity, finance a useful asset, bridge timing, or create access that would be difficult to arrange later. It can also magnify a weak cash-flow system and turn a market decline into a forced sale.

Strategic debt starts after the household proves four things:

1. The current bills are affordable.
2. The Reserve is sufficient for the added obligation.
3. The stressed leverage remains survivable.
4. The repayment source exists before the money is borrowed.

## Strategic use 1 — Preserve liquidity

Paying cash removes the payment but also removes the cash.

Financing can be useful when the purchase is necessary and keeping liquidity protects the household from another risk. A family may finance part of a vehicle, business equipment, or home project rather than empty the Reserve or sell a low-basis asset.

The comparison is:

- financing cost;
- value of retained liquidity;
- payment pressure;
- flexibility to prepay;
- collateral at risk.

## Strategic use 2 — Keep low-rate debt while capital does another job

Paying off debt creates a guaranteed return equal to the avoided cost. Keeping the debt preserves capital for another use.

The spread is expected, not guaranteed. It only helps when:

- the payment remains comfortable;
- the Reserve is intact;
- the investment horizon is long enough;
- the household can hold through underperformance;
- the financing terms do not reset unexpectedly.

## Strategic use 3 — Finance a large purchase

Large purchases can be funded with:

- cash;
- a taxable asset sale;
- conventional financing;
- home equity;
- business financing;
- seller financing;
- a securities-backed line;
- a retirement-plan loan where available;
- Bitcoin-backed borrowing.

The cheapest stated rate does not automatically create the safest plan. Each source places a different asset, income stream, or future contribution at risk.

## Strategic use 4 — Acquire a productive or appreciating asset

A household may consider financing:

- a business;
- equipment that produces income;
- real estate;
- education that increases earning power;
- Bitcoin or another long-term asset.

The return case has to include the financing cost, the cash-flow burden, the downside, and the exit.

For Bitcoin, the household should model a deep drawdown immediately after the purchase. The question is whether the debt can be carried while the purchased asset is worth much less than the amount invested.

## Strategic use 5 — Establish optional access before it is needed

A line of credit arranged while income, credit, and collateral are strong may create useful optionality later.

Availability is not a command to borrow. The line needs its own rule:

- permitted purposes;
- maximum amount;
- repayment source;
- expiration or reset risk;
- who can authorize use;
- no-go conditions.

## Home equity

Home equity can support a large purchase or investment without selling another asset. It also places the family home inside the risk chain.

Compare:

- payment and rate behavior;
- total claim on the home;
- whether the rate can reset;
- what happens if income falls;
- whether the investment can be held through a long drawdown;
- whether the family would still feel secure living in the home.

An HEI or shared-appreciation structure may reduce or remove a monthly payment while giving up part of future home appreciation or creating a later settlement obligation. The contract determines the actual economics.

## Bitcoin-backed borrowing

Bitcoin-backed borrowing can provide liquidity without an immediate sale. It introduces:

- interest cost;
- lender custody;
- LTV movement;
- margin or collateral calls;
- forced liquidation;
- repayment risk;
- contract and counterparty risk.

The full provider and threshold work stays in Advanced. The core test is whether the household can survive a severe Bitcoin decline without losing the collateral or raiding the rest of the plan.

## Continuing household comparison

Alex and Morgan are considering a home renovation and a larger Bitcoin purchase. They compare:

- paying cash from the Reserve;
- selling taxable investments;
- increasing the HELOC;
- using conventional project financing;
- taking a Bitcoin-backed loan;
- reducing the project or delaying it;
- financing the renovation while keeping the Bitcoin purchase funded only from future surplus.

The best comparison may produce two separate decisions rather than one large loan.

## Done when

- Every proposed use of debt has a specific purpose.
- The repayment source is named.
- The collateral and failure mechanism are understood.
- The household has modeled the bad version, not only the expected return.
- Strategic borrowing is either allowed under written rules or intentionally excluded.

# 3.5 — Compare financing choices for one large decision

**Target runtime:** 6–8 minutes  
**Member decision:** Which funding method does the least damage to the rest of the plan?

## The comparison table

For one real or hypothetical large purchase, compare the same fields across every funding source:

| Funding source | Up-front cash used | Monthly payment | Variable-rate risk | Asset or collateral at risk | Tax effect | Forced-action risk | Exit / repayment |
|---|---:|---:|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cash |  | 0 | None | Liquidity leaves |  | Low | Complete at purchase |
| Taxable sale |  | 0 | None | Growth asset sold | Gain / loss | Low | Complete at sale |
| Conventional loan |  |  | Possible | Purchased asset / guarantee |  | Payment default | Amortization / payoff |
| Home equity |  |  | Often | Home |  | Home and reset risk | Payment / settlement |
| Business loan |  |  | Possible | Business / guarantee |  | Business cash flow | Operating cash flow |
| Securities-backed |  |  | Often | Investment account |  | Collateral call | Sale / repayment |
| Retirement-plan loan |  |  | Contract-specific | Future retirement contributions / employment |  | Job-change rules | Payroll / payoff |
| Bitcoin-backed |  |  | Contract-specific | Bitcoin with lender | Sale may be deferred at origination | Liquidation | Cash, refinance, or collateral sale |

The member should not fill every row for every purchase. Use the table when the decision is large enough to move retirement timing, taxes, the Reserve, or household risk.

## The five questions

1. What is the total cost if the asset or project produces no financial return?
2. Which balance sheet is being pledged?
3. What happens after an income drop or rate reset?
4. What creates a forced sale or forced repayment?
5. Which option leaves the rest of the plan easiest to follow?

## Member action

Choose one large purchase, investment, or financing idea. Compare at least three realistic funding methods. Keep the idea in Scenarios until the household has actually committed.

## Done when

- The comparison uses the same purchase amount and timing.
- Taxes, interest, collateral, and payment pressure are shown separately.
- The household can explain why one option fits better than the others.
- The saved baseline remains unchanged until the decision is real.

# 3.6 — Write the household leverage rules before borrowing

**Target runtime:** 5–7 minutes  
**Member decision:** What rules must every new loan pass?

## The leverage policy

Write the rules while the household is calm.

### Purpose

Allowed uses might include:

- a necessary large purchase;
- a temporary timing bridge;
- productive business investment;
- an asset acquisition that survives the stress test.

List any excluded uses, such as covering a recurring deficit or chasing a rising market.

### Payment

Set the maximum payment burden the household can carry alongside normal living costs, required saving, and the Reserve.

### Balance sheet

Set both a current and stressed debt-to-assets range.

### Collateral

Set the maximum share of the home, securities, or Bitcoin the household is willing to place in a lender's control.

### Repayment

Name the primary and backup repayment sources. “The asset will go up” is not a repayment source.

### Triggers

Define what causes review or reduction:

- income drop;
- variable-rate reset;
- collateral value decline;
- Reserve below target;
- upcoming large expense;
- lender or contract change.

### Monitoring

Name who checks the loan and how often. Fast-moving collateralized debt may require more attention than a fixed mortgage.

## Continuing household

Alex and Morgan allow strategic borrowing only when:

- the Reserve remains above its floor;
- the payment fits one income if needed;
- stressed debt-to-assets stays inside their chosen range;
- the home or Bitcoin collateral exposure is acceptable to both spouses;
- the repayment does not depend only on selling the purchased asset at a higher price;
- the decision improves the full plan in Preview.

## Done when

- The household has one written debt and leverage policy.
- Every new loan must pass the same questions.
- The policy includes no-go conditions.
- Both spouses can explain the largest risk.

# 3.7 — Guided working session: give every debt and credit line a job

**Format:** replaceable Orange Plan walkthrough  
**Estimated capture:** 18–25 minutes  
**Capture gate:** verify every route, label, calculation, and Current-versus-Preview behavior against the approved redesign commit used for filming

## Before the member starts

Have available:

- lender statements or current online balances;
- rates and whether they are fixed or variable;
- minimum payments;
- remaining terms or payoff dates;
- collateral and lender thresholds;
- unused home-equity or other credit facilities;
- one large purchase or strategic borrowing idea worth testing.

## Step 1 — Verify current debt facts

**Destination:** Home → Debt monitoring or the final canonical debt entry path.

For every row:

- confirm balance;
- confirm rate;
- confirm payment;
- confirm type and collateral;
- confirm payoff, reset, or maturity date;
- mark missing or stale information.

**Read what changed:** current debt total, required monthly burden, and any Needs Attention item.

## Step 2 — Read payment pressure

Open the canonical Debt and leverage workspace from Plan → Build & improve → Improve strategy.

Read:

- total required payment;
- debt-to-income;
- which payment creates the most cash-flow pressure;
- whether a variable rate changes the forward plan.

## Step 3 — Read current and stressed leverage

Show current debt-to-assets, then run the approved drawdown comparison.

Read:

- asset value before and after;
- debt that remains;
- stressed debt-to-assets;
- collateral or LTV warnings where supported.

## Step 4 — Assign each debt a job

For every debt choose:

- minimum;
- extra;
- refinance;
- consolidate;
- restructure;
- pay off;
- monitor.

Record the reason and review trigger.

## Step 5 — Set the extra-debt claim

Enter the total monthly extra payment the household intends to make now.

Return to Cash Flow and confirm that the amount reduces the same monthly surplus that later funds contributions.

## Step 6 — Preview one strategic financing decision

Use Scenarios or the dedicated strategy comparison to test one large decision:

- cash versus financing a vehicle or project;
- home equity versus a taxable sale;
- business financing versus personal funds;
- selling Bitcoin versus borrowing against it;
- borrowing to acquire an asset versus funding it from future surplus.

Keep it focused on one decision.

Read:

- payment;
- tax effect;
- Reserve impact;
- stressed leverage;
- retirement timing;
- chance of success, with simulation count beneath it;
- collateral and forced-action risk.

Do not save the scenario unless the household has actually committed.

## Step 7 — Record the leverage policy

Write or save:

- allowed purposes;
- payment ceiling;
- current and stressed leverage range;
- collateral limits;
- repayment sources;
- triggers;
- no-go conditions;
- review cadence.

## Step 8 — Create the outside action list

Examples:

- schedule the card payoff;
- call the lender for current variable-rate terms;
- request refinance disclosures;
- confirm unused HELOC terms;
- gather a loan contract for professional review;
- automate the extra payment;
- decline or delay the proposed leverage;
- return to Allocation with the final investable surplus.

## Session checkpoint

The session is complete when:

- every debt and credit facility is current;
- debt-to-income and current / stressed debt-to-assets are understood;
- every debt has one job and reason;
- the extra-debt amount appears in Cash Flow;
- strategic borrowing is either excluded or governed by written rules;
- the member has no more than three immediate outside actions;
- Session 4 can route the remaining monthly surplus without a provisional debt number.
