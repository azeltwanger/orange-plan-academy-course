11.3 How to read a financial plan
~8 min at 155 wpm · SPOKEN-PROSE VERSION (calibrated)
============================================================

In today's lesson, we're going to cover how to actually read a financial plan, which is the skill this whole course has been building toward.

A plan report gets read in the order that answers 4 questions. Where do you stand today? That's position. Where are you headed? That's trajectory. What could break it? That's risk. And what do we do about it? Those are the actions. Every number in your report belongs to one of those four.

And the order matters, because each question depends on the one before it. Trajectory is computed off position. Risk is your trajectory tested against assumptions you didn't pick. Actions come out of what the risk read found. Starting in the middle means trusting numbers you never checked.

== 1. POSITION ==

Position is the honest snapshot of today. Your net worth, what's in Bitcoin versus everything else, and what you owe.

For the couple, that's the $30,000 of cash, the 300 shares of the index fund, the Bitcoin across both wallets, and the $298,000 of debt underneath it all.

There's no judgment at this layer yet. You're just confirming the picture is true today. And this is where stale data gets caught. Reading position first is what stops a single wrong account from corrupting every number downstream. The question to ask: if you moved that quarter Bitcoin to cold storage last month, does the report know?

== 2. TRAJECTORY ==

Trajectory is two numbers that get read together, always as a pair.

The earliest-retirement date tells you when. And the confidence number tells you how sturdy that date is. It's the share of simulated futures where the plan funds your spending all the way through.

Here's why the pairing matters. Age 60 at 82% confidence is a real answer. Age 57 at 55% confidence would be a prettier date and a worse plan. Because a date on its own can always be moved earlier by assuming better returns. The confidence number is what surfaces the cost of that assumption.

== 3. RISK ==

Risk is the "what if I'm wrong" sections. The different Bitcoin paths, the spending band, and the protection lines.

Let me talk about the band, because it changes how you think about spending. In a real plan, spending isn't one number. The floor is what the plan asks you to live on in a bad stretch. The ceiling is what it lets you spend when things go well. The couple's $80,000 sits in a band with a floor around $72,000 and a ceiling around $88,000.

And you ask one question of every risk section: would I still be okay in this scenario? For the couple, the whole risk read is one sentence: on the Bitcoin path they did not pick, does the plan still hold at $72,000? If yes, they're okay. That's it. You're not picking the prettiest line. You're checking survival.

== 4. ACTIONS ==

Actions are the shortest section, and they're where the review actually changes anything.

The rule from the maintenance module applies here too: one to three finishable actions, and nothing more. For the couple that's: finish the cold storage move, top up the reserve, and take one conversion question to the CPA. All three finishable before the next monthly pass.

== THE SIX RED FLAGS ==

Now, the two-minute scan. Six red flags, and you can check for all of them fast.

One: a position section that doesn't match reality, because stale balances make every number after them wrong.

Two: a date you like at a confidence you don't. That's a plan that only works if you get lucky.

Three: a spending number with no band around it. A single number gives you nothing to fall back to in a bad year.

Four: a Bitcoin-path section you've never actually looked at, because that section is what tells you what happens when your main assumption is wrong.

Five: a next-steps list longer than three items. Long lists rarely get finished.

And six: an assumptions section you couldn't defend out loud. Every number in the document rests on it.

If you find any of these, the review isn't finished yet.

The standard for this module is simple: you can answer all 4 questions out loud, in four sentences, about your own report.

== SAVE ONE PDF PER YEAR ==

Next habit: save one PDF per year, right after your annual review, while the data is fresh. Year in the filename, saved somewhere it will still exist in 10 years.

That document does its real work the second time you save one, when there are two of them side by side.

== THE YEAR-OVER-YEAR RE-READ ==

Because a price change by itself can't tell you whether the plan got better.

Take a rally year. Bitcoin's worth $200,000 more, and it feels great. But spending drifted from $80,000 to $88,000 and nobody entered it, the reserve never got topped back up, and the tax question never got asked. The rally moved one number and showed you none of that.

I lived the other direction. In 2022, my net worth dropped 75%. On price alone, that's a catastrophe. But my income hadn't changed, my spending hadn't changed, and the date barely moved. A report from that year would have shown me all three of those things in a couple of minutes, while the price chart was showing me nothing but red.

So with two reports side by side, you look at four lines, in order.

First, net worth and the Bitcoin share. If the share went up while you only made small buys, price is the reason, which is drift you didn't choose. If it went up because you bought more, that's a decision you made. Two different stories, same number.

Second, the date and confidence, as a pair. The same date at higher confidence is real improvement. An earlier date at lower confidence means an assumption moved, not the plan.

Third, the spending band, and especially the floor. The floor is the honest read, because it's the number you're stuck with in a bad stretch. A rising floor tells you the plan got stronger. A rising ceiling on its own doesn't.

And fourth, last year's action list. Did each one actually happen? Anything unfinished rolls forward.

== THREE HANDOFFS ==

One more thing about the report: you're not the only reader. There are three handoffs.

The spouse sit-down, where the report is your agenda. Where you stand today, the date, the band. Done in twenty minutes.

The CPA, who gets the tax pages plus the transaction export, so they can price a conversion or a harvest instead of reconstructing your year from statements.

And the estate attorney, who gets the access and estate pages plus your decisions, so the drafting conversation starts from what you already decided.

Notice what you're doing in all three: you're handing each professional a document, not asking them to log into an app. That's what lets three different professionals start from the same set of facts.

== THE WHOLE POINT ==

You started this course with accounts scattered everywhere and a rough guess. You're finishing with a document your family can read, your CPA can act on, and next-year-you can be measured against.

That finished document is what a financial plan actually is.
