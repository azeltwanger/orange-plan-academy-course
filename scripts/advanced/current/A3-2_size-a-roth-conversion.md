> **Watch this only if the household has meaningful traditional retirement money and a lower-income window worth comparing. Otherwise record that no conversion review is needed now.**

The question is not, “How much can I convert?”

It is: **how much ordinary income room is actually available, what else changes when we use it, and is paying this tax now better than the alternative?**

== START WITH THE YEAR, NOT THE ACCOUNT BALANCE ==

A conversion is added to the tax year that receives it.

Begin with the income already expected:

- wages or business income,
- pension and Social Security,
- interest and dividends,
- traditional withdrawals,
- capital gains that interact with the return,
- and other modeled income.

Then identify the bracket or tax ceiling the household is considering.

The difference between the ceiling and the income already using it is the first estimate of conversion room. It is not the final amount.

== WORK AN ILLUSTRATIVE WINDOW ==

Assume a retired household expects $40,000 of ordinary income before a conversion and is considering a planning ceiling of $80,000.

The first-pass room is:

> $80,000 − $40,000 = **$40,000**

A $40,000 conversion moves that amount from traditional to Roth and generally adds $40,000 of ordinary income in the current year.

That looks simple. The full decision is not.

The additional income can change:

- federal and state tax,
- taxation of Social Security,
- Marketplace premium-tax-credit eligibility,
- Medicare-related costs in the applicable years,
- capital-gain room,
- estimated payments or withholding,
- and the cash needed to pay tax.

I would treat the $40,000 as a ceiling to compare—not a recommendation to fill automatically.

The correct amount may be smaller or zero.

== COMPARE NOW WITH LATER ==

A conversion is useful when the current marginal cost is acceptable relative to the future cost it may prevent.

The future comparison includes:

- projected traditional balance,
- future withdrawals or required distributions,
- later Social Security and pension income,
- future filing status,
- state residence,
- estate and beneficiary goals,
- and the value of Roth flexibility.

Do not convert simply because the current bracket is low. The future rate may be equal or lower. The account may already be modest, or another income-based cost may make the current year unattractive.

== PAYING THE TAX IS PART OF THE STRATEGY ==

If the household converts $40,000 and owes an additional $8,000 of combined tax, where does the $8,000 come from?

Paying from accessible cash generally preserves more of the converted amount in Roth.

Paying tax from the retirement account can reduce the amount converted and may create additional issues depending on age and account rules.

The tax money cannot also be the emergency reserve or next year's Bridge without deliberately weakening those jobs.

== WATCH THE MULTIPLE FIVE-YEAR RULES ==

Roth accounts have more than one five-year concept. The result depends on account type, age, distribution type, and the source of the money.

Core should not teach a shortcut such as “wait five years and every Roth dollar is available.”

The CPA and maintained reference confirm how conversion principal, earnings, and prior Roth history apply to the household.

== USE A CEILING, NOT A FALSE PRECISE ANSWER ==

The app can preview a conversion amount or fill toward a ceiling.

Treat that as a comparison range.

Run at least:

- no conversion,
- a smaller conversion,
- and the proposed ceiling amount.

Read current tax, future traditional balance, future required distributions, confidence, Bridge liquidity, and ending account mix.

A lower lifetime-tax estimate is useful. It is not enough when the strategy creates a cash problem or depends on a law assumption decades away.

== WHERE THE NUMBERS COME FROM ==

### Conversion room

**What it means:** amount of ordinary-income capacity remaining under the selected planning ceiling before other interactions.

**Calculated from:** ceiling minus modeled ordinary income already using it.

**Edit source:** income, filing status, state, ceiling, and conversion settings.

### Conversion tax cost

**What it means:** additional modeled tax caused by the conversion in the selected year.

**Calculated from:** the full tax result with conversion minus the result without it.

**This affects:** cash, tax, Roth and traditional balances, future distributions, healthcare costs, and estate flexibility.
