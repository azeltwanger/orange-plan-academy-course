> **Watch this only if you plan to retire before your retirement accounts are freely accessible or you cannot explain which account funds each early year. Otherwise the Core Bridge lesson is enough.**

The question is not, “How do I get around age 59½?”

It is: **which dollars are genuinely available in each early-retirement year, what tax or penalty applies, and which backup source keeps the household from forcing the wrong withdrawal?**

== ACCESS, TAX, AND PENALTY ARE DIFFERENT ==

An account can be accessible and still taxable.

A distribution can be penalty-free and still create ordinary income.

A Roth withdrawal can contain contribution basis, conversion dollars, and earnings. Each can have different ordering and five-year rules.

An HSA can be available for qualified medical expenses without being a general spending account.

Do not label an entire account “locked” or “tax-free” from one headline rule.

Map the source, amount, tax treatment, additional-tax treatment, plan restriction, and records required.

== START WITH THE YEAR-BY-YEAR GAP ==

The demo household retires when Alex is 55.

== THE AGE-55 EMPLOYER-PLAN EXCEPTION ==

Under current federal rules, some qualified-plan distributions can avoid the 10% additional early-distribution tax after separation from service. The separation generally has to occur in or after the year the employee reaches 55.

The exception generally does not follow the money into an IRA.

That is why an immediate rollover of the entire current-employer plan can remove an option the household expected to use.

But the exception is not a command to spend from the plan.

Pre-tax money is generally still taxable when distributed. The employer plan also controls whether partial or periodic withdrawals are available.

For the demo, Alex retires during the year Alex reaches 55. The current employer plan may be a Bridge option when the plan document and administrator support the needed distributions.

Jordan's older or former-employer accounts do not automatically inherit Alex's exception.

Verify the exact plan before the retirement date and before any rollover.

== ROTH IRA CONTRIBUTION BASIS ==

Under current Roth IRA ordering rules, regular contributions are treated as distributed before conversions and earnings.

That can make documented contribution basis an accessible Bridge source.

The difficult part is the record.

A current Roth balance does not show how much regular contribution basis remains after prior distributions. Conversions have their own ordering and additional-tax periods. Earnings have qualified-distribution rules.

The household needs Forms 5498, prior returns and Forms 8606 when relevant, account history, and a CPA-reviewed basis schedule.

Do not enter the full Roth balance as accessible because the account is called Roth.

== 72(t) / SUBSTANTIALLY EQUAL PERIODIC PAYMENTS ==

A substantially equal periodic payment plan can create an exception to the additional early-distribution tax under current rules.

It is not a flexible checking account.

The calculation method, payment schedule, account balance, start date, and modification rules matter. Changing the series incorrectly can create retroactive tax and interest consequences.

I would treat this as a professional-only tool when taxable Bridge, employer-plan access, Roth basis, or another simpler source does not solve the gap.

The app can model the income stream. The CPA or retirement-plan specialist designs and verifies the actual series.

== HSA AND OTHER ACCOUNT-SPECIFIC SOURCES ==

An HSA can reimburse qualified medical expenses tax-free when the expenses and records qualify under current rules.

For the demo, that makes the HSA part of the Healthcare Bridge—not unrestricted lifestyle spending.

A governmental 457(b), pension, inherited account, or another plan can have its own access rules. Use the actual account document rather than assuming every retirement wrapper follows the same age.

== BUILD THE ACCESS WATERFALL BY CONSTRAINT ==

I would read the sources in this order:

1. **Accessible without special machinery:** cash and taxable Bridge
2. **Account-specific qualified source:** HSA for qualified expenses or verified current-employer-plan access
3. **Documented Roth contribution basis:** only the amount actually supported
4. **Designed exception strategy:** 72(t) or another current-law exception after professional review
5. **Plan change:** reduce spending, work longer, or change the retirement date when the Bridge is too thin

The order is not universal. Use the simplest source that preserves the rest of the plan, and know the tax cost before the withdrawal.
