# Orange Plan Academy — learning-design evidence

**Purpose:** explain why the repaired course teaches one decision, demonstrates it on one continuous household, asks the learner to explain the number, and then has them complete the analogous work in Orange Plan.

This file supports the structure. It is not permission to turn the course into academic prose. The learner should experience the design rather than hear a lecture about instructional science.

## 1. Use one worked household before asking the learner to solve the whole plan

Worked-example research found that novice learners can learn more efficiently from studying a correctly solved example than from being asked to discover every step through unguided problem solving. The advantage is strongest when the example directs attention toward the structure of the problem instead of forcing the learner to search blindly for a solution.

**Course application**

- Use one continuous fictional household across Core.
- Explain the decision and work it once before asking the learner to repeat it in their own plan.
- Keep the demo complex enough to be realistic but not crowded with every edge case.
- Move optional borrowing, complex custody, advanced tax execution, and trusts behind plan-visible gates.
- Fade support over time: early walkthroughs explain every important number; later walkthroughs ask the learner to predict the source and result first.

**Source starters**

- Sweller, J., & Cooper, G. A. (1985). *The use of worked examples as a substitute for problem solving in learning algebra.* Cognition and Instruction, 2(1), 59–89. https://doi.org/10.1207/s1532690xci0201_3
- Atkinson, R. K., Derry, S. J., Renkl, A., & Wortham, D. (2000). *Learning from examples: Instructional principles from the worked examples research.* Review of Educational Research, 70(2), 181–214. https://doi.org/10.3102/00346543070002181

## 2. Prompt self-explanation instead of adding another summary paragraph

Learners who explain why steps work and connect them with the underlying principle tend to build a more usable mental model than learners who only reread a finished example.

**Course application**

At the first important output in each module, the learner answers:

1. What does this number mean?
2. What is it calculated from?
3. Where is the source edited?
4. What else should move when it changes?

The walkthrough should pause before revealing a result and ask the learner to predict what should change. The course does not need a second long recap when the learner can explain the relationship themselves.

**Source starter**

- Chi, M. T. H., Bassok, M., Lewis, M. W., Reimann, P., & Glaser, R. (1989). *Self-explanations: How students study and use examples in learning to solve problems.* Cognitive Science, 13(2), 145–182. https://doi.org/10.1207/s15516709cog1302_1

## 3. Retrieve the decision and number source, not isolated vocabulary

Retrieval practice improves long-term retention more reliably than repeated study alone when the learner must bring the information back to mind and receives an opportunity to correct the answer.

**Course application**

End each concept lesson with a brief retrieval action tied to the plan:

- state the decision in one sentence,
- identify the source number,
- predict the output that should move,
- and then verify it in the app.

The Academy does not need a separate trivia quiz after every video. The retrieval task is the financial-planning action itself.

During later modules, briefly retrieve earlier concepts where they are used:

- recall Plan confidence before teaching spending guardrails,
- recall tax pools before choosing withdrawal sources,
- recall custody roles before connecting legal authority,
- recall Baseline versus Scenario before the final comparison.

**Source starters**

- Roediger, H. L., III, & Karpicke, J. D. (2006). *Test-enhanced learning: Taking memory tests improves long-term retention.* Psychological Science, 17(3), 249–255. https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-9280.2006.01693.x
- Karpicke, J. D., & Blunt, J. R. (2011). *Retrieval practice produces more learning than elaborative studying with concept mapping.* Science, 331(6018), 772–775. https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1199327

## 4. Segment the teaching around decisions rather than screens

Multimedia-learning experiments have found that learners can understand a complex explanation better when it is broken into learner-paced, meaningful segments rather than delivered as one uninterrupted presentation.

**Course application**

- One lesson owns one main decision.
- One primary visual makes that decision or relationship obvious.
- The concept video remains evergreen.
- The current app walkthrough is a separate replaceable segment.
- Long changing procedures, current limits, and provider-specific instructions live in lesson text or maintained reference.
- The learner can pause after the concept, make the decision, and then enter the walkthrough.

The course should not segment so aggressively that the learner loses the whole plan. The same demo household and Build Your Plan crosswalk preserve continuity between the smaller lessons.

**Source starter**

- Mayer, R. E., & Chandler, P. (2001). *When learning is just a click away: Does simple user interaction foster deeper understanding of multimedia messages?* Journal of Educational Psychology, 93(2), 390–397. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.93.2.390

## 5. Make the subgoal visible

Learners transfer a procedure more effectively when they can see the functional subgoals rather than memorizing a surface sequence of clicks.

**Course application**

Walkthroughs are organized around durable subgoals:

- verify the source,
- enter or apply the decision,
- read the result,
- trace the number,
- and confirm completion.

Do not teach a screen sequence as though it were the financial concept. A route can change while the planning subgoal remains the same.

Build Your Plan is the navigation spine for these subgoals. Exact buttons and routes remain versioned walkthrough metadata.

**Source starter**

- Catrambone, R. (1995). *Aiding subgoal learning: Effects on transfer.* Journal of Educational Psychology, 87(1), 5–17. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.87.1.5

## 6. Turn a decision into a specific implementation action

Research on implementation intentions distinguishes a general goal from a concrete plan linking a situation with an action. The Academy's goal is not merely for a learner to prefer a strategy; it is to know what happens next, who owns it, and when it will be reviewed.

**Course application**

Every planning area ends with:

- the chosen decision,
- the app or provider source where it is implemented,
- the finish line,
- an owner,
- and a date when an outside action is required.

The final plan carries only one to three current actions rather than an unowned list of twenty possible improvements.

**Source starter**

- Gollwitzer, P. M. (1999). *Implementation intentions: Strong effects of simple plans.* American Psychologist, 54(7), 493–503. https://doi.org/10.1037/0003-066X.54.7.493

# Structural decisions supported by this evidence

## Keep

- concept first, then worked demo, then analogous learner action,
- one continuous household,
- one decision per lesson,
- number provenance,
- brief prediction and teach-back prompts,
- current app feedback,
- versioned walkthroughs,
- one to three next actions,
- and the six-sentence final plan summary.

## Avoid

- a click tour before the learner knows the decision,
- a new generic example in every lesson,
- long passive recaps,
- trivia quizzes disconnected from the plan,
- every Advanced edge case inside Core,
- a screen label treated as the durable concept,
- or a large assignment with no immediate source or feedback.

# Pilot implications

The course should be tested on what learners can do, not only whether they liked the presentation.

The first pilot measures whether learners can:

- state the decision,
- explain the important number,
- find the source,
- predict the downstream movement,
- complete the app task,
- and explain why the final choice fits.

Use `PILOT-TEST-PLAN.md` for the actual protocol and pass thresholds.

# Source maintenance

These papers support broad instructional principles. They do not validate every Orange Plan recommendation, app calculation, or financial-planning claim.

Tax, legal, custody, insurance, lending, and software behavior use their own current professional and product sources. The instructional evidence informs how those verified concepts are taught.
