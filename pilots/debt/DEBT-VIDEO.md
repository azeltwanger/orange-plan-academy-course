## 1. Decide what debt is doing for you

Should you pay off your mortgage, keep buying Bitcoin, or consider borrowing more?

Those can all be reasonable questions. But before you compare the interest rate with what you think Bitcoin might earn, you need to know what the debt does to the rest of your life.

Alex and Morgan, our example household, owe $13,500 on a credit card at 20.9%. They also owe $318,000 on a mortgage at a fixed 3.25%.

The mortgage is the bigger balance. The card is the more expensive debt. And paying either one down uses money that could go toward their cash reserve or other goals. We need a way to decide which job matters most.

By the end of this section, you'll have a debt strategy: what you're keeping, what you're paying down, and the conditions you'd need to meet before borrowing more. I'll explain the process first. Then we'll bring it into Orange Plan. You can watch the example through or pause as you work on your own plan.

I think about debt the way a business owner thinks about financing a project. They want to know what the money costs, what it could help them accomplish, and whether they can carry the payments through a bad period.

Let's say a business can borrow $100,000 at 5% and has a project it expects to earn 18% on. The borrowing costs $5,000 a year if the balance stays the same. The expected return is $18,000. That's a reason to investigate the project.

But the business still needs to fund the interest and repay the $100,000 if the project disappoints. The expected return alone doesn't decide it.

Your household can think through the same questions. There are three reasons I might keep an affordable loan instead of rush to pay it off.

The first is liquidity. That's money you can access when you need it.

An extra mortgage payment moves cash into home equity. You still have that value, but getting it back could mean selling the home or qualifying for another loan. If the family relies on one income, keeping some cash available may be more useful than reducing a low-rate mortgage faster.

The second is the return you expect from investing the difference.

Put an extra $10,000 toward a 4% loan, and you avoid roughly $400 of annual interest while that amount would otherwise remain outstanding. Invest the $10,000 instead, and it could earn more. It could also lose value. That's the trade-off before taxes and fees.

My Bitcoin conviction is a reason to compare investing with paying down inexpensive debt. It isn't a reason to assume every loan is worth keeping. I still want the payments covered without needing Bitcoin to go up next month.

The third is avoiding a sale today. A loan can let you keep an appreciated investment instead of selling it to fund a purchase. That can postpone realizing a taxable gain. Whether it saves money depends on the interest, fees, tax on the actual gain, and eventual repayment. We'll use the full tax comparison in the retirement-income section.

And some people simply don't want debt. I understand that. If being debt-free helps you sleep and follow your plan, that's a real benefit. You don't have to borrow just because another strategy looks better in a projection.

So start with the purpose. Is the debt funding your home, preserving cash, or helping you acquire an asset? Or is it covering spending that your income can't support? Those are different problems, and they lead to different decisions.

## 2. Measure your household's financial health

There are two ratios I use to get a starting picture. One measures what you owe against what you own. The other measures the payments against your income.

Debt-to-assets is total debt divided by total asset value. If you owe $200,000 and own $800,000, that's 25%.

Use the asset value before subtracting debt. Net worth is what remains after the subtraction.

The Reeds' example balance sheet has $1,996,000 in assets and $444,500 of debt. That's about 22% debt-to-assets and $1.55 million of net worth.

We're using the course's simplified balance sheet. It includes their home and retirement accounts, but leaves out vehicle and business-equipment values while keeping the related loans. Use a consistent asset list when you compare the numbers over time.

Now ask which assets could actually support the debt. A valuable house can make the ratio look good without giving you cash for next month's bills. Retirement accounts and education money may have access restrictions or other commitments. Keep those separate from resources you're willing and able to use.

The second measure is debt-to-income, usually called DTI. Add the required monthly debt payments and divide them by gross monthly income, before taxes and payroll deductions. Extra payments you choose to make are a separate decision.

For example, $2,000 of required monthly payments against $8,000 of gross income is 25% DTI.

The Reeds' listed loan payments total about $3,342 a month. Their household income is about $19,417 a month before household taxes. That includes Morgan's business income after ordinary operating costs, with the equipment-loan payment counted separately. We're comparing income available to the household, not total business sales.

Their payment ratio is about 17%. This is our planning calculation from the listed loans. A mortgage lender can also include housing taxes, insurance, and other obligations, so it isn't a qualification result.

Seventeen percent sounds low. But after their original living costs, taxes, and Alex's existing retirement contribution, they only have $500 left each month.

That tells us something the ratio doesn't. Most of the income is already being used somewhere else.

You need both views. Debt-to-assets helps you see leverage. DTI helps you see required payment pressure. Actual cash flow tells you what remains for saving, extra payments, and living your life.

## 3. Choose a working range

Now that you have the numbers, what should you aim for?

For this lesson, we're using a set of review bands as starting points. They help identify where to look more closely. They don't establish a safe borrowing amount for every household.

For debt-to-assets, below 30% is the lower-leverage range. From 30% through 60% is the caution range. Above 60% is high leverage: you owe more than $60 for every $100 of assets in the calculation.

For payments against gross income, below 36% is the lower-pressure range. From 36% through 43%, a larger share of your income is committed. Above 43%, debt payments are taking a substantial amount before taxes and ordinary living expenses.

These are planning guideposts, not loan-approval rules. The most a lender will offer and the most you're comfortable carrying can be very different amounts.

I'd start by checking whether your existing obligations fit below those first review points, then tighten your own limit based on your household. Being below a number doesn't mean you should borrow until you reach it. Zero debt is fine too.

Income stability is a big part of that choice. Two reliable incomes may give a family options if one job ends. A single income based on commissions needs more room for a weak period. Two jobs tied to the same slowdown may offer less protection than two genuinely different income sources.

Then look at the people and costs the money supports. Dependents, large fixed bills, and a long time needed to replace lost income are reasons to consider less debt and more accessible cash.

Life stage matters too. A payment that fits while you're working needs another look before you retire. At that point, we test it against the income and asset withdrawals your retirement plan will provide.

Here's how I'd turn that into a limit rather than just a feeling. In a separate illustration, a household with $8,000 of monthly gross income chooses to test a 25% payment limit. That would allow $2,000 of required payments in total.

It already pays $1,600, so the ratio leaves $400 of room. But after living costs and its existing saving commitments, it only has $250 available. A proposed $350 payment doesn't fit unless the household deliberately changes something else.

The personal limit and the cash-flow limit both matter. Use the tighter one. Then test whether it still works when conditions get worse.

The same idea applies to debt-to-assets. With $800,000 of assets, a chosen 25% ceiling is $200,000 of total debt. That's a limit the household is testing, not a recommendation to borrow $200,000. For a new loan, calculate the assets and debt after the transaction as well. Borrowed cash is an asset until you spend it; an investment bought with it can rise or fall.

Now return to the Reeds. Their ratios are about 22% and 17%, both in the lower bands. Yet they have expensive card debt, only $500 of original monthly surplus, and an unfinished reserve.

I wouldn't use those low percentages as a reason to add a loan. Their cash flow and cash protection need attention first.

Your working range should leave room for the plan you want to follow. If reaching the top would mean stopping retirement saving or worrying about every unexpected bill, choose a lower limit.

## 4. Test the range through a difficult period

A debt level that looks comfortable after Bitcoin has risen can look very different after it falls. The balance you owe doesn't fall with it.

Let's stress the Reeds' same asset list. Bitcoin exposure falls 70%, the selected stock holdings fall 30%, and the home falls 20%. We're holding the education and healthcare accounts unchanged in this illustration.

Their included assets fall from $1,996,000 to $1,217,200. Debt stays at $444,500. The ratio rises from about 22% to about 37%.

They've moved into the caution range without borrowing another dollar.

That doesn't automatically mean they should sell Bitcoin. I'd look next at whether the payments can continue and whether any loan requires a response to falling collateral.

If their income and required payments stay the same, the payment ratio stays around 17%. If income falls by half and payments don't change, it rises to roughly 34%. A weak business period can happen at the same time as an investment decline.

Their reserve is $32,000. In the earlier reserve example, essential outflows are $7,200 a month, including required debt payments. If no income continues, the reserve covers about four and a half months.

They're testing a six-month target of $43,200, so they're $11,200 short. If some income continues, compare it with the bills that remain and calculate the smaller gap the reserve has to cover.

This is how the stress test changes a decision. A household that can keep making payments without exhausting its resources may choose to retain an affordable loan. A household that needs a fast recovery or another loan just to keep paying the bills needs less debt, lower spending, or more cash protection before adding anything optional.

Also test a slow recovery. A short drop and several years of weak returns are different problems when interest keeps running. Use the actual repayment date. A plan that works in five years doesn't solve a balance due in twelve months.

With Bitcoin, I also pay attention to where the price is relative to where it's been. After a strong rally, I'd rather preserve borrowing room than treat the higher asset value as permission to add obligations.

Let's say someone earns $8,000 a month and has a $1,200 mortgage payment. That's 15% of gross income. During a rally they add another $2,000 of monthly loan payments. Now they're at 40%.

If Bitcoin falls, the payments don't go back to $1,200. The person kept the higher obligation even though the asset value that made them comfortable has disappeared.

That's why I'd be more restrained after a big run. After a decline, someone who kept their cash flow and reserves in good shape may have a reason to compare borrowing. But the lower price doesn't prove the decline is over. I would test another difficult period from that price before making the decision.

So when is it worth moving forward with a loan comparison? When the borrowing has a clear purpose, the payments fit, and the downside test leaves the household able to cover its needs. You also need a way to repay the balance and, where applicable, meet the lender's collateral requirements.

For the Reeds, the immediate priority is improving cash flow, funding the reserve, and reducing the card. A new optional loan can wait. Someone whose plan passes those checks can move on to comparing specific financing.

## 5. Compare the loan structures

Two loans for the same amount can put very different pressure on your household. Start with the payment, then the rate and what happens if you can't follow the agreement.

Let's borrow $20,000 at 8% for five years, with no fees.

A fully amortizing loan costs about $406 a month. That means the scheduled payments gradually pay down the balance as well as interest, until the loan is paid off.

An interest-only version at the same rate costs about $133 a month while the balance stays at $20,000. You have roughly $272 more flexibility each month, using the unrounded payments.

I like that flexibility when there's a clear plan for the principal. It can let you keep cash available or invest, with the option to repay sooner if the agreement allows it.

But after five years of interest-only payments, you still owe the original $20,000. You would have paid $8,000 in interest, compared with about $4,332 on the amortizing version. Someone who wants a scheduled path out of debt may prefer the higher payment that steadily pays it off.

The rate matters because it changes the cost of keeping the money invested. So do fees, a variable rate, and the payment after an introductory or interest-only period ends. Compare the whole agreement, including the balance due at the end.

Collateral is the next question. A conventional mortgage is secured by the home, but it doesn't normally have a daily price-based margin call. Investment-backed borrowing can require more collateral or repayment when values fall. An unsecured personal loan avoids that collateral call, but missing payments can still lead to collection and legal consequences.

Here's the order I generally investigate financing. It's an order for comparing the choices, not a guarantee that one loan type is always cheaper or safer.

First, the low-rate mortgage you already have. If you're making extra payments, consider whether redirecting those extras would fund the goal. Keeping the mortgage doesn't give you a lump sum today, but it can change where future cash goes.

A home-equity line may be another option. You can draw as needed under its terms, and some offer interest-only payments during the draw period. Your home secures it. Check the rate, when repayment changes, and when the lender can reduce or freeze unused access. An available line isn't the same as cash already in your reserve.

Next, consider an eligible securities-backed line. It can provide access to cash without selling the pledged investments, but falling values can bring a collateral demand or sale. A non-purpose line generally can't fund purchases or trades in securities. Check the permitted use of the actual loan before counting on it.

An unsecured personal loan can let you keep Bitcoin outside a lending arrangement. Compare the required payment and total cost. The lack of Bitcoin collateral doesn't make the debt disappear if you run into trouble.

Then there's Bitcoin-backed borrowing, which adds a collateral-management responsibility we'll work through in a moment.

Credit cards and other high-rate debt are the last place I'd look for long-term financing. I'd usually direct extra payments there before assuming Bitcoin's return will outrun the cost.

You can investigate borrowing access while your income is strong without deciding to use it. Include setup costs and any required initial draw. You're creating an option, not a reason to spend.

For a business purchase, start with the cash left after operating costs and working-capital needs. That is what would support the payment. A personal guarantee can bring the obligation back to the household. A seller-financed deal, retirement-plan loan, or shared-appreciation agreement needs its own terms compared before you choose it.

### Bitcoin-backed loans: size the debt before posting collateral

*[Optional chapter within this video. Members without a Bitcoin-backed loan or borrowing plan may skip to Chapter 6.]*

I can see a place for a Bitcoin-backed loan when the household can support the debt and is comfortable with the lender risk. Before I take one, I want to know how much Bitcoin I'd need if the price fell 80%.

Then I decide how much collateral to send. I might open the chosen loan at 50% LTV so I put less Bitcoin with the lender, leaving the rest under my control for top-ups. That's a higher-risk starting position that needs active management.

LTV is the loan balance divided by the value of the collateral actually posted. It isn't the percentage of your total Bitcoin that you've placed with a lender.

Let's use a separate example. Bitcoin starts at $100,000. You have 3.5 BTC set aside to support one loan, including what you'll initially pledge and what you can add later.

First assume an 80% liquidation threshold, no stricter cure or maturity limit, and interest and fees paid from cash flow we've already funded. We'll change those terms in a moment.

An 80% price decline leaves Bitcoin at $20,000. Your 3.5 BTC would be worth $70,000. A $50,000 loan would then be at about 71.4% LTV if all the collateral had been posted in time.

To find the exact liquidation boundary, divide $50,000 by 80%. That requires $62,500 of collateral. At $20,000 per Bitcoin, that's 3.125 BTC. Exactly that amount reaches the liquidation line. You need more to stay below it.

You can also work backward from the coins available. An 80% decline leaves 20% of their starting value. Multiply that by an 80% liquidation threshold and you get 16% of their value today. On $350,000 of supporting Bitcoin, that's a $56,000 debt boundary.

The example's $50,000 is below that boundary only while the balance stays at $50,000. One hypothetical year of 12% interest added at year-end takes the debt to $56,000. Against $70,000 of stressed collateral, that's exactly 80% LTV. All $6,000 of room is gone.

So interest added to the balance needs to be included before you choose the loan amount.

Now change the contract. Say a margin call requires you to restore LTV to 65% by a deadline. That's called curing the call. Remaining below 80% doesn't satisfy that requirement.

At our stressed price, $50,000 of debt needs about $76,923 of collateral to reach 65%. That's approximately 3.85 BTC in total, not 3.5. If the debt has grown to $56,000, you'd need about 4.31 BTC to reach 65%.

The original amount doesn't fit that contract with the coins we've set aside. We need to borrow less, dedicate more Bitcoin, or have a separately funded way to repay part of the balance.

Let's borrow less. With 3.5 BTC worth $70,000 after the decline, a 65% requirement allows $45,500 of outstanding debt at that point. Allowing for one year of the example's 12% added interest brings the opening balance at that boundary to $40,625.

I'd leave room below it. To illustrate, choose $35,000 instead. After that year of interest, the balance is $39,200. Against $70,000 of collateral, that's 56% LTV, below the 65% cure level. That extra room is a choice in this example, not a universal safe loan size.

Now decide what to post at the beginning. A $35,000 loan at 50% LTV needs $70,000 of collateral. At our starting Bitcoin price, that's 0.7 BTC. The other 2.8 BTC stays in cold storage, available for this loan.

Posting all 3.5 BTC upfront would start the same $35,000 loan at 10% LTV. You'd have more room for a decline without doing anything, but all those coins would already be with the lender. Starting at 50% leaves more under your control and gives you more responsibility for adding it when needed.

Notice what we did. We sized the debt using the downside, interest, and contract requirements. Only then did we choose the initial collateral deposit. We didn't borrow half the value of every Bitcoin we owned.

The cold-storage coins must actually be available. You can't reserve them for two loans or count retirement-account Bitcoin you can't transfer under the loan's terms. And you need to remain comfortable if the full amount eventually has to be pledged. Lower LTV doesn't remove the risk of the lending arrangement itself.

Also, you have to act during the decline. At 50% starting LTV, a 37.5% drop in the posted collateral reaches an 80% liquidation line when the balance stays fixed. The 80% drawdown is the scenario you're preparing for, not the point when you start transferring coins.

Choose a response point well before the contract forces action. Know how much you'd add or repay and allow time for access, transfer, and the lender to credit it. A fast drop or an outage can leave less time than you expect.

An automatic top-up feature may need Bitcoin in an account with the provider. It doesn't simply take coins from your cold wallet. That can help with response time, but it changes where you hold the reserve. Check the actual setup and what happens when you're asleep or unable to act.

The loan also needs a repayment plan. If the principal comes due before your expected sale, the timing doesn't work. Renewal may have different terms, and some agreements can force a sale at maturity or after a missed payment even while LTV is below the headline liquidation threshold.

If you plan to borrow again later, rerun the calculation for all the debt and the remaining available coins. Interest and new borrowing both use capacity. You can't treat the reserve used by the first loan as spare protection for the second.

An 80% stress test isn't a maximum possible loss or a promise against liquidation. It tells you what the collateral could support under the assumptions. You still need to be comfortable with the interest, the repayment, the provider, and the effect on the rest of the household.

## 6. Put the decision into your plan and follow it

Let's turn the framework into a decision for the Reeds, then apply the same process to your plan.

I'd give their 20.9% card priority for extra payments. Keep required payments covered on every loan. For the 3.25% fixed mortgage, I'd stay with the scheduled payment while the card and reserve need money.

Their 6.7% car loan and 7.4% equipment loan are decisions to revisit after those more urgent jobs. The car payment is $600 and the equipment payment is $480. Clearing either could improve monthly flexibility, but using a large amount of cash now would slow the reserve and card payoff.

The $46,000 home-equity line needs a repayment plan too. At the example's 8% rate, roughly $307 a month covers interest while leaving the balance unchanged. I wouldn't add an optional draw before knowing when that payment changes and how they'll repay the principal.

They're also considering a $30,000 renovation. Taking it from the $32,000 reserve would leave $2,000. Borrowing could preserve the cash but add another obligation. Selling an investment would use an asset and may create tax on its gain. For them, I'd keep a smaller project or waiting in the comparison, and leave the optional borrowing out for now.

Here's the pace they're considering. Their proposed $1,200 spending reduction takes available monthly cash from $500 to $1,700, after taxes, required debt payments, and Alex's current contribution.

Of that $1,700, they're considering $500 for the reserve and $1,200 extra for the card. The card's $405 required payment is already in their bills. Adding the $1,200 makes its total payment $1,605.

What does that accomplish? In a simplified payoff calculation, keeping the payment at $405 takes about 51 months and costs about $6,879 in interest. Paying $1,605 takes about 10 months and costs about $1,226 in interest. That's about $5,653 less interest.

Those figures assume a fixed 20.9% rate, monthly interest, payments at month-end, and no new purchases or fees. They compare two fixed payment amounts; they aren't a prediction of a card's changing minimum payment.

That's a meaningful use for the extra money. But the spending reduction has to happen for this pace to be real. And if the family needs cash protection faster, I'd shift more toward the reserve for a while and reduce the extra card payment. Required payments continue either way.
