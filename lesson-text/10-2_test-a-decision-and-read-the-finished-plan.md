# Test a decision and read the finished plan

Your plan answers one question: if things go roughly the way you assumed, here is where you land. A scenario answers a different one: **what happens if they don't?** One baseline plan that represents your real life, and a set of saved what-ifs beside it you can compare against one at a time, without disturbing the plan.

## Life event or scenario?

- **Life event**: something you genuinely expect. Tuition in eight years, a roof, a car, retirement itself. Goes **in the plan**, because leaving it out makes the projection wrong.
- **Scenario**: something you are considering or worried about. Retiring three years earlier, moving to a no-tax state, Bitcoin dropping 50% right after you retire. Goes **in Scenarios**, because putting it in the plan corrupts the baseline.

**The test:** am I telling the plan what's true, or asking the plan a question? True things go in the plan. Questions go in Scenarios.

The common way people wreck their own baseline: get curious about retiring at 55, change the retirement age in the plan "just to see," get distracted, and three months later read a projection built on a decision they never made.

## The two kinds of what-if

| Kind | Asks | What you do with the answer |
|---|---|---|
| **Stress test** | Would I survive this? | Survive it, or go fix something |
| **Choice** | Should I do this? | Weigh it, decide, then act or drop it |

## Choosing the scenario type

Pick the type that matches your question and you get the controls that matter instead of all forty.

| Type | What it covers |
|---|---|
| **Life events** | Income, spending, housing, family, one-time events |
| **Retirement** | Timing, spending, income, Social Security |
| **Savings** | Contribution amounts, routing, future savings mix |
| **Debt** | Existing debts, new loans, BTC borrowing |
| **Tax** | State, Social Security, dividends, Roth conversions |
| **Markets** | Return assumptions, asset reallocations |
| **Advanced** | Every scenario control at once |

Advanced is for genuine multi-part scenarios. Reach for it last, not first.

## Change one thing at a time

Retire three years earlier *and* cut spending *and* assume weaker Bitcoin returns, and a worse result teaches you nothing. You cannot tell which one did it, or whether two cancelled out. **One variable per scenario.** Three changes together is a fourth scenario, read after you understand each piece alone.

## Reading the comparison honestly

1. **Read the pair, not the number.** Two years earlier at a much lower success rate is a different trade, not a better plan.
2. **Run the Monte Carlo before you believe the success rate.** The chart updates instantly; the simulation has to actually run.
3. **Small differences aren't differences.** 2% from baseline means the change didn't matter. Don't reorganize your life around noise.

## When a scenario graduates

A scenario that has become a decision is no longer a scenario: change the actual plan. A real decision left in Scenarios means your baseline is wrong in a way you will forget about. The reverse counts too: a stress test the plan survives is completed work, not a failure.

**Two worth keeping permanently:** a deep drawdown right after retirement (the sequence-of-returns test, the single scenario most likely to break a Bitcoin-heavy plan), and weaker Bitcoin returns than you assumed. Both feed the "if Bitcoin follows a different path" section of your yearly report.

## Reading the plan: four questions, in order

| # | Question | Section |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Where do you stand today? | Position |
| 2 | Where are you headed? | Trajectory |
| 3 | What could break it? | Risk |
| 4 | What do we do about it? | Actions |

Each question depends on the one before it. Trajectory is computed off position; risk is trajectory tested against assumptions you didn't pick; actions come out of the risk read. Starting in the middle means trusting numbers you never checked.

**Position**: net worth, Bitcoin versus everything else, what you owe. No judgment yet. This is where stale data gets caught, and reading it first stops one wrong account from corrupting everything downstream.

**Trajectory**: the earliest-retirement date and the confidence number, always as a pair. Age 60 at 82% is a real answer. Age 57 at 55% is a prettier date and a worse plan, because a date alone can always be moved earlier by assuming better returns.

**Risk**: the Bitcoin paths, the spending band, the protection lines. Spending is not one number. The floor is what the plan asks you to live on in a bad stretch; the ceiling is what it lets you spend when things go well. $80,000 sits in a band with a floor of ~$72,000 and a ceiling of ~$88,000. One question of every risk section: would I still be okay here?

**Actions**: one to three finishable items. The only place the review changes anything.

## The six red flags

1. A position section that doesn't match reality
2. A date you like at a confidence you don't
3. A spending number with no band around it
4. A Bitcoin-path section you have never actually looked at
5. A next-steps list longer than three items
6. An assumptions section you couldn't defend out loud

**The standard:** you can answer all four questions out loud, in four sentences, about your own report.

## Save one PDF per year

Right after the annual review, while the data is fresh. Year in the filename, somewhere it will still exist in ten years. It does its real work the second time you save one, because a price change by itself cannot tell you whether the plan got better.

A rally year: Bitcoin worth $200,000 more and it feels great, while spending drifted $80k to $88k unentered, the reserve never got topped up, and the tax question never got asked. The rally moved one number and showed you none of that.

With two reports side by side, read four lines in order:

1. **Net worth and Bitcoin share.** Share up on small buys means price did it, which is drift you didn't choose. Share up because you bought is a decision you made.
2. **Date and confidence, as a pair.** Same date at higher confidence is real improvement. Earlier date at lower confidence means an assumption moved, not the plan.
3. **The spending band, especially the floor.** A rising floor means the plan got stronger. A rising ceiling on its own doesn't.
4. **Last year's action list.** Anything unfinished rolls forward.

## Three handoffs

- **Spouse**: the report is the agenda. Where you stand, the date, the band. Twenty minutes.
- **CPA**: the tax pages plus the transaction export, so they price a conversion instead of reconstructing your year from statements.
- **Estate attorney**: the access and estate pages plus your decisions, so drafting starts from what you already decided.

In all three you hand a professional a document rather than asking them to log into an app. That is what lets three different people start from the same set of facts.
