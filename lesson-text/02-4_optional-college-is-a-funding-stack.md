# Optional: college is a funding stack

> **This lesson is optional.** It applies if you have children, or another education goal you expect to help pay for. If you don't, skip it — your Module 2 plan is complete without it, and nothing later in the course depends on it.

Same six questions as the future-cost lesson, applied to the one dated cost where getting it wrong is most expensive.

## College is a funding stack, not a bill you prepay

College is one of the easiest goals to overfund, because people start with the sticker price and assume the parents are responsible for all of it. **That is the wrong starting point.** Not because full funding is bad, but because the final school, net price, aid package, student contribution and family contribution are all still uncertain.

The published price and what families actually pay are very different numbers. Most first-time full-time students receive some grant aid, and average net tuition after grants is far below the published figure at both public and private schools.

⚠ **Check the current figures against the schools your child is actually considering.** For reference, 2025-26 averages: published tuition and fees ~$11,950 (public four-year, in-state) and ~$45,000 (private nonprofit); estimated average net tuition after grants ~$2,300 and ~$16,910 respectively. **These change every year, they are national averages rather than a quote for any named school, and they are not spoken in the video for that reason.**

### Step 1: define your commitment

Decide what you are actually promising, before calculating anything:

- A fixed amount per child
- Four years at an in-state public benchmark
- Tuition only, with the student covering housing
- A percentage of the final net cost
- A fixed annual contribution
- Whatever the existing 529 plus a set amount of cash flow provides

**This is a family-value decision.** The plan should never silently assume three children means four private-college sticker prices each.

### Step 2: build the stack

    Projected school cost
    − grants and scholarships
    − student work and other student resources
    = REMAINING NET COST

The remaining net cost divides among six sources:

1. Existing 529 savings
2. New 529 contributions
3. Parent cash flow **while the child is enrolled** (the one people forget)
4. Planned Bitcoin or taxable-asset sales
5. A limited amount of student borrowing
6. Any remaining unfunded amount

Not every family uses all six. **College does not have to be solved entirely with money saved before freshman year.**

A complete plan might be: the parents provide the existing 529, add a fixed amount from annual cash flow, the student applies for aid and works summers and takes a defined amount of federal loans, and Bitcoin covers part of the rest if the price and the tax situation work out. That is a finished plan. It is not a failure because the 529 doesn't equal four years of sticker price.

## Where Bitcoin fits

**The goal is not to stop buying Bitcoin for seven years because your child may go to college.** The goal is that the amount you have *firmly promised* does not depend entirely on Bitcoin being at a favourable price on the exact day tuition is due.

| When | What you do |
|---|---|
| **5+ years out** | Keep accumulating; contribute a reasonable amount to the 529. Do not try to solve an uncertain four-year sticker price now |
| **2–4 years out** | Update likely schools, net prices, 529 balance, aid eligibility, student willingness to work or borrow, and your commitment. Begin protecting **part** of the committed amount |
| **Final 1–2 years** | Make sure year one, maybe year two, of what you promised has a reliable source: 529, cash, cash flow, or a Bitcoin sale made during a strong market. Later years stay flexible |
| **While enrolled** | Recalculate annually after the actual aid package arrives |

That is what gives Bitcoin time to compound without putting you in a spot where a 70% drawdown forces you to sell at the bottom.

## Where the 529 fits

Use the 529 for the portion of your commitment you are reasonably confident will be spent on qualified education. Preserve flexibility for the rest.

Its flexibility features soften modest overfunding without justifying blind overfunding: qualified distributions are generally federal-income-tax-free; the beneficiary can generally be changed to another family member; a lifetime amount can go toward qualified student-loan principal or interest; and limited unused funds may roll to the beneficiary's Roth IRA subject to annual, lifetime, account-age and contribution-history conditions.

⚠ **Every figure and condition above is set by statute and changes.** Verify current rules before acting. Aid coordination — including how education accounts are reported on the FAFSA for the student versus for siblings — belongs here and in the app, not in a recorded video.

## Financing part of it is not a failure

A modest amount of federal student debt can give the student ownership of the decision, protect parental retirement flexibility, avoid a forced Bitcoin sale in a bear market, and spread the cost across the years the education is producing income. And if Bitcoin does what you think it will, you can pay it down later.

**Two different decisions:**

- **Student federal loans**: bounded and controllable, a legitimate part of the stack
- **Parent or private borrowing**: a separate debt-policy decision, modelled against retirement, interest cost and cash flow like any other debt

⚠ Loan limits change (parent PLUS rules changed 1 July 2026, among others). Verify current limits rather than relying on any figure written here.

What you decide is the *shape*: we are comfortable with our student borrowing up to some amount, if the payment is reasonable for the career they are heading into. We are not signing up for unlimited parent debt.

## Aid and school choice are part of the plan

File the FAFSA every year. Apply for state, school and outside scholarships. **Compare net price, not sticker price.** Compare graduation rates, expected earnings and likely debt. Request an aid adjustment if family finances materially change, and reconsider when a package makes a school uneconomic.

**The core rule:** a known college expense needs a *funding plan*, not necessarily full pre-funding. **A college goal inside ten years does not automatically mean you stop buying Bitcoin until the full projected cost is sitting in cash or a 529.**

## Your decision

What you are actually committing to provide. A fixed amount per child, four years of in-state tuition, tuition only, a percentage of the final net cost, or whatever the 529 plus a set amount of cash flow can carry. **That is a family decision, and nothing in the plan should make it for you.**

## Put it in Orange Plan

Same two places as the future-cost lesson, with one difference that matters.

| What | Where | The college difference |
|---|---|---|
| **The cost** | Plan → Retirement → **Life events** → Add event. Name, year, amount; mark it recurring for the four years of tuition | **The amount you enter is your commitment, not the sticker price.** If you have promised $30,000 a child, that is the number. The rest of the stack lives outside the plan by design |
| **The container** | Strategy → Allocation → raise the **Bridge** target | So the money lives somewhere that will not be down 40% the year the bill lands |
| **The monthly amount** | Cash Flow → **Routing** | Whatever closes the gap between the 529's projected value and the part of your commitment you have decided to protect — usually a much smaller number than people expect |

## You are done when

You can say what you are committing to provide, name the sources that will cover it, and point at the part you have decided to protect from a Bitcoin sale. **You are not done when the whole sticker price is sitting in cash.** That was never the target.
