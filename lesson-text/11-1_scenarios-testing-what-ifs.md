# Scenarios: testing what-ifs against your plan

Your plan answers one question: if things go roughly as assumed, here's where you land. A scenario answers a different one: what happens if they don't. You keep one baseline plan that reflects your real life, and a set of saved what-ifs beside it you can compare against one at a time, without disturbing the plan.

## Life event or scenario?

| | Goes where | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Life event** (something you expect) | In the plan | College tuition in 8 years, a roof, a car, retirement |
| **Scenario** (something you're considering or worried about) | In Scenarios | Retiring 3 years earlier, moving states, a 50% drawdown |

The test: **am I telling the plan what's true, or asking it a question?** True things go in the plan; questions go in Scenarios.

⚠ The most common way people wreck their own baseline: getting curious about retiring at 55, changing the retirement age *in the plan* to see, then forgetting. Three months later they're reading a projection built on a decision they never made.

## The two kinds of what-if

- **Stress tests** ask *would I survive this?* Bear markets, a 50% drawdown right after retirement, weaker Bitcoin returns. You're not planning for these, you're checking that the plan holds anyway. One question only: would I still be okay?
- **Choices** ask *should I do this?* Retire earlier, spend less, move states, claim Social Security at 62 instead of 70. Here you're comparing two futures and picking one.

A stress test you either survive or go fix. A choice you weigh, decide, then act on or drop.

## Choosing the scenario type

The app asks what kind of scenario it is before anything else. Picking the right type shows you the controls that matter instead of all of them.

| Type | Covers |
|---|---|
| Life events | Income, spending, housing, family, one-time events |
| Retirement | Timing, spending, income, Social Security |
| Savings | Contribution amounts, routing, future savings mix |
| Debt | Existing debts, new loans, BTC borrowing |
| Tax | State, Social Security, dividends, Roth conversions |
| Markets | Return assumptions and asset reallocations |
| Advanced | Every scenario control at once |

Start with the specific type. **Advanced** is for genuine multi-part scenarios, so reach for it last.

## Change one thing at a time

A scenario that retires you earlier *and* cuts spending *and* assumes weaker returns teaches you nothing when it comes back worse. You can't tell which change did it, or whether two cancelled out. **One variable per scenario.** Want to know what three changes do together? That's a fourth scenario, read after you understand each piece alone.

## Reading the comparison honestly

Headline row: **Retire at**, **Spending**, **Success rate**. Below it, full comparison metrics: net worth at retirement, lifetime taxes paid, effective tax rate, withdrawal rate at retirement, depletion age, BTC at life expectancy.

1. **Read the pair, not the number.** Retiring two years earlier at a much lower success rate is a trade, and every scenario is a trade.
2. **Run the Monte Carlo before believing the success rate.** The chart updates instantly; confidence is a simulation that has to actually run.
3. **Small differences aren't differences.** A scenario landing 2% from baseline is telling you the change didn't matter. Don't reorganize your life around noise.

## When a scenario graduates

When a scenario stops being a question and becomes a decision, go change the actual plan, then delete the scenario or keep it as the record of what convinced you. Leaving a real decision in Scenarios means your baseline is now wrong in a way you'll forget.

The reverse: a stress test the plan survives is completed work, not a failure. Save it and move on.

## Two worth keeping permanently

- **A deep drawdown right after retirement.** The sequence-of-returns test, and the scenario most likely to break a Bitcoin-heavy plan. Re-run it yearly.
- **Weaker Bitcoin returns than you assumed.** If the plan only works at your optimistic curve, that's worth knowing every year.

Both also populate the report's "If Bitcoin follows a different path" section.

## Homework

1. Save one stress test: a deep drawdown right after your retirement year. Run the Monte Carlo.
2. Save one choice you're genuinely weighing, as a single-variable scenario.
3. Answer the stress test out loud. Would you still be okay? If no, build the lever you'd pull as its own scenario and see whether it fixes it.
