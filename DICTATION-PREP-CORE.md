# Orange Plan Academy — core pre-dictation guide

This is the file to work from **before** opening the long teleprompter draft.

It is not finished Austin dictation. It is a cleaned starting point designed to make dictation faster: a natural opening, the order the explanation should follow, the examples and qualifications that cannot get lost, and a simple handoff to the walkthrough. The longer scripts remain the research reference.

## The rule for this pass

- Austin's real dictation stays the authority.
- A teach lesson explains the decision, why it matters, and the number or rule the learner should write down.
- The module walkthrough owns the click path and data entry.
- Do not force every lesson into the same hook, three-part payoff, `Your decision`, `Put it in Orange Plan`, and `You are done when` speech pattern.
- Do not add a clever closing line merely because the script feels like it should land harder.
- Keep factual qualifications from the research audit, but say them in plain language.

## 0.2 · How to use Orange Plan AI

### Start from

> In today's lesson, we're going to walk through how to use the AI inside Orange Plan and some of the ways it can help while you're building and maintaining your plan.

### Cover it in this order

1. Start with the orange **AI Review** button. It opens Plan Guide from any page. The first option changes based on the page because the AI is being given the part of the plan that matches the decision the user is working on.
2. Give a few useful prompts instead of reading every available button:
   - What changed after I updated these numbers?
   - What information is missing or stale?
   - What am I not thinking about before I make this decision?
   - Compare these two choices and explain the trade-offs.
   - What should I model before I take this to my CPA, attorney, or planner?
3. Explain the division of labor. The app calculates the surplus, taxes, LTV, retirement date, confidence, and other outputs. The AI reads those results, helps explain them, and helps find missing information. It is not rebuilding the financial engine inside the chat.
4. Explain the daily Bitcoin report as a quick market read: current price across several windows, distance from the prior high, ETF and public-company activity, leverage, on-chain changes, macro conditions, and material industry news. The useful question at the end is whether any of it changes the user's actual financial plan. Most days it should not.
5. Explain how it becomes more useful as the plan gets more complete. After accounts, spending, debts, allocation, taxes, or retirement income change, ask what moved and which input is driving the result.
6. Show the privacy-scrubbed Markdown or JSON export for someone who would rather use ChatGPT, Claude, or another AI.
7. End with the one safety rule: no seed phrase, private key, wallet backup, passphrase, PIN, password, full account number, Social Security number, or backup-file passphrase goes into any AI.

### Leave out

- A long list of what AI cannot do.
- Every page's exact button copy.
- Repeating a legal-sounding disclaimer after each example.

### Handoff

Have the learner run one page-specific review, run the daily Bitcoin report once, and locate the AI export. There is no separate screen walkthrough for Module 0.

## 1.3 · Read your starting retirement date and the confidence number you will run later

A full cleaned working draft is already in the canonical 1.3 script. The main correction is that onboarding uses **one deterministic projection under the selected assumptions**. It is not an average of many paths and it does not run Monte Carlo.

### Start from

> In today's lesson, we're going to cover what the retirement age you saw at the end of onboarding actually means, because I don't want you to look at that number like Orange Plan already built your entire financial plan.

### Cover it in this order

1. Onboarding used rough information: age, income, spending, broad asset amounts, where most non-Bitcoin assets live, and the selected Bitcoin model. It did not have every account, debt, future expense, tax record, Social Security input, or withdrawal decision.
2. Orange Plan takes that one set of inputs and assumptions, projects the plan year by year, checks different retirement ages, and finds the earliest age where the projection lasts through the life-expectancy assumption.
3. There are not 1,000 paths at this point. That is why the age changes directly when the user switches the Bitcoin growth model.
4. The number is still useful. It tells the user whether retirement looks roughly close or far away and shows how much the assumptions affect the answer. It is not the finished retirement date.
5. Confidence waits because a percentage based on rough accounts and rough spending would look more precise than the information underneath it.
6. After the full plan is built, the app runs 1,000 market paths. If 820 fund the plan as written, the result is 82%. That does not mean there is exactly an 18% probability of going broke. It means the other modeled paths required an adjustment.
7. The finished earliest-retirement date is the first age that clears the confidence target the user chooses, not simply the first age where the deterministic projection works.

### Handoff

Ask the learner to write down the onboarding age and the rough input most likely to change. Then send them to the Foundation walkthrough to add real accounts and current holdings and review the assumptions. Do not tell them to enter income, debt, life events, Social Security, tax history, or run the full confidence check here.

# Module 2 — Cash Flow and Reserve

## 2.1 · Find your surplus and your two spending numbers

### Start from

> In today's lesson, we're going to figure out how much money you actually have left every month and the two spending numbers the rest of your plan is going to use.

### Cover it in this order

1. Remove money that is already spoken for. Tax reserves and bills landing soon are not emergency savings. This matters most for self-employed households because the cash may be sitting in the same account.
2. Define surplus as income minus taxes, living expenses, and minimum debt payments. Retirement contributions are not subtracted yet because where the surplus goes is a later decision.
3. Use the couple's example: $190,000 income minus $40,000 taxes, $80,000 living, and $22,000 debt payments leaves $48,000 per year, or about $4,000 per month.
4. Slow down on the spending input. Living spending is not gross income and it does not include the separate debt-payment row. A bad spending number corrupts the retirement date, reserve, and withdrawal plan.
5. Run **Keep, Cut, Reduce**. Keep what is necessary or worth it. Cut what is not being used. Reduce the things the household wants to keep but can pay less for.
6. After the easy cuts, name the large levers: housing, vehicles, and location usually matter more than subscriptions.
7. Create two spending numbers. Normal spending is the current lifestyle. Bare-bones spending is what the household could live on in a bad year. The reserve is sized from the bare-bones number.
8. Check withholding and new income. Over-withholding hides monthly surplus; under-withholding creates a later bill. Give raises and bonuses a job before they disappear into lifestyle.

### Keep or cut deliberately

The 2022 personal drawdown example supports the cash-flow point. The campsite story is more distracting than necessary unless Austin specifically wants it.

### Handoff

Have the learner write down normal spending, bare-bones spending, and true monthly surplus. The walkthrough shows where to enter the numbers, how to link or import real transactions, and how to verify the result.

## 2.3 · Fund a known future cost: the six questions

### Start from

> In today's lesson, we're going to cover how to plan for expenses you already know are coming so they do not drain your emergency reserve or force a Bitcoin sale at the wrong time.

### Cover it in this order

1. Separate known costs from emergencies. College, a roof, a vehicle, a renovation, and planned taxes belong in the plan because the household can see them coming. The emergency reserve is for what it could not predict.
2. Explain the funding lane. The closer the date, the less volatility the committed money can carry.
3. Inside one year: cash-like assets. One to three years: short-term, low-volatility assets. Three to five years: begin separating the firmly committed portion from the flexible portion.
4. State Austin's ruling clearly: from five to ten years out, Bitcoin can remain part of the funding plan. There is no automatic rule that the whole expense has to move into cash at year five.
5. As the date gets closer, the portion the household absolutely cannot afford to miss should become less dependent on Bitcoin. The flexible portion may remain exposed longer. In the final one or two years, the committed amount needs a reliable funding source.
6. Ask the six questions:
   - What are you actually committing to provide?
   - What is already saved?
   - What can cash flow cover at the time?
   - What can reasonably be financed?
   - What can come from Bitcoin or other investments?
   - What portion has to stop depending on Bitcoin as the date gets close?
7. Avoid the conventional overcorrection. A future expense needs a funding plan. It does not automatically need to be fully prefunded before the household continues buying Bitcoin or working on every other goal.

### Handoff

The life event records the expected cost. Allocation records the amount being protected in Bridge. Cash Flow records the monthly routing amount. Let the walkthrough perform those clicks.

## 2.4 · Optional: college is a funding stack

### Start from

> This lesson is optional. It is for anyone who expects to help pay for a child's or another family member's education. If that does not apply, skip it.

### Cover it in this order

1. Decide the promise before calculating the savings target. Four years of in-state tuition, a fixed dollar amount, whatever is in the 529 plus a cash-flow amount, or a percentage of the final net cost can all be valid commitments.
2. Do not start with sticker price. The family does not yet know the school, aid package, student contribution, or whether the student will attend. The plan should not silently assume the parents cover every published dollar.
3. Build the funding stack: projected school cost minus grants, scholarships, and student work leaves the remaining net cost. That remainder can be covered by the current 529, new 529 contributions, cash flow while enrolled, planned taxable or Bitcoin sales, bounded student borrowing, and anything left unfunded.
4. Explain where Bitcoin fits over time. Five or more years out, Bitcoin can remain part of the plan while the family makes reasonable 529 contributions. Two to four years out, start protecting the portion the family has firmly promised. Inside two years, committed tuition should not depend on Bitcoin recovering on schedule.
5. Protect retirement first. The parent can borrow for education; the parent cannot borrow for retirement. A college target that breaks the household's retirement plan is too large.
6. Use the couple's numbers only to demonstrate the stack. Do not present one universal amount every parent should fund.

### Handoff

The learner writes down what the family is actually promising and which parts of the funding stack will cover it. The Module 2 walkthrough enters the life event and funding amount.

# Module 3 — Debt Strategy

## 3.1 · Give every debt a job and set your ceiling

### Start from

> In today's lesson, we're going to decide which debts you keep, which ones you pay down faster, and the total level of debt you are not willing to go past.

### Cover it in this order

1. Start with tolerance. Ratios create a range; they do not measure how debt affects the household emotionally. If zero debt is what lets someone follow the plan through a drawdown, that is a legitimate answer.
2. Explain why debt can also be useful. Cheap debt can preserve liquidity and keep invested capital working. The payment still has to fit comfortably inside cash flow.
3. Use two ratios for different jobs:
   - DTI: monthly debt payments divided by gross monthly income. This measures monthly pressure.
   - DTA: total debt divided by total assets. This measures how much of the balance sheet is already spoken for and moves when Bitcoin moves.
4. Use the couple's example. Their DTI is low while DTA sits near the top of the workable range. Both can be true at the same time.
5. Separate three questions that are often blended together: keep stacking, pay existing debt faster, and add new leverage. They can have different answers.
6. Give each debt an app job: minimum only, extra principal, refinance, consolidate, pay off in full, or monitor.
7. Use the rate tiers as a starting point, not a moral rule: over 10% usually deserves elimination; 7% to 10% needs evaluation; under 7% may be strategic; asset-backed debt is monitored by its collateral and LTV rules.
8. Show why two debts can receive the same job for different reasons. The job needs a reason attached to it.

### Avoid

- "Three decisions wearing one coat."
- Treating the rate bands as universal law.
- Saying the ceiling is an app field when it is a household policy.

### Handoff

The learner writes down the job and reason for every debt and the ceiling they will not cross. The walkthrough enters the debts and records the strategies.

# Module 4 — Allocation and the Next Dollar

## 4.1 · Set the Bitcoin allocation you can hold through a full drawdown

### Start from

> In today's lesson, we're going to decide how much Bitcoin you can actually hold and still follow the plan during a full bear market.

### Cover it in this order

1. Define the denominator. Bitcoin may be 60% of investable assets, 39% of net worth, and 24% of gross assets for the same household. Use investable assets for the allocation tier and the other numbers as context.
2. Explain what sets a responsible allocation: education, conviction built from that education, and what the person would actually do during a major drawdown.
3. Introduce the allocation tiers as shorthand, not identities someone earns.
4. Run the stress test in dollars. A 75% Bitcoin drawdown multiplied by the allocation shows the approximate portfolio hit. The dollar loss is what the household will feel.
5. Use the couple's example. Show the Bitcoin balance and investable portfolio after the drawdown while the mortgage, car payment, and groceries remain unchanged.
6. The honest question is whether the user could watch that loss without selling. If the answer is no, lower the position until the answer becomes believable.
7. Compare allocation level with custody level. If the position has grown beyond what the custody setup can protect and the family can recover, custody has to catch up.

### Avoid

- Brave-sounding payoff lines about what happens at 3 a.m.
- Presenting historical drawdowns as a guaranteed maximum.
- Repeating the full app stress-test click path.

### Handoff

Have the learner write down the largest Bitcoin allocation they believe they could hold through a 70% to 80% drawdown. The Module 4 walkthrough saves and tests it.

## 4.2 · Break your accounts down by holding type, then by timeframe

### Start from

> In today's lesson, we're going to look at the same portfolio two different ways: what you own and when you are going to need the money.

### Cover it in this order

1. First, look through the account wrappers and total the actual asset classes: Bitcoin, stocks, bonds, real estate, cash, and other. An account name does not tell the user what is inside it.
2. Use the couple's accounts to show how stocks can live in several wrappers and one account can contain more than one asset type.
3. Set a target mix and a drift band. The target guides new money and measures drift; it is not automatically an instruction to sell today. A band keeps every price move from becoming a decision.
4. Then sort the accounts by when the money will be used:
   - Reserve: near-term, low-volatility money.
   - Bridge: accessible money for intermediate goals and the years before other income or accounts are available.
   - Legacy: long-duration money that has time to carry volatility.
5. Let the job decide the bucket. An HSA, taxable account, or another wrapper can serve different timeframes depending on how the household plans to use it.
6. The app may infer a timeframe from the account type. Treat that as a starting guess, not the household's final decision.
7. Explain where the targets come from: Reserve from the reserve policy, Bridge from known costs and retirement bridge years, and Legacy from what remains.

### Handoff

Have the learner write down the target mix and which accounts belong to Reserve, Bridge, and Legacy. The walkthrough assigns them and shows what is funded or behind.

## 4.3 · Order your contributions: which account gets funded first

**Blocked for Austin's planning rule. Do not dictate the existing strict waterfall.**

The cleaned shape is:

1. State the **default order**.
2. Name the items that usually come first, such as an employer match or very high-interest debt.
3. Name the facts that override the default: an underfunded pre-59½ Bridge, variable income, near-term retirement, a thin reserve, a current tax window, or another real constraint.
4. Explain how HSA, Roth, traditional, taxable Bitcoin, and taxable Bridge assets are compared when more than one is valid.
5. Explain when deliberately splitting the next dollar is the right answer rather than indecision.

> Once the reserve and debt policy are set, the default route for the next dollar is ________. The things that usually come first are ________. I would override that order when ________. A deliberate split makes sense when ________.

## 4.4 · Asset location: which account each holding belongs in

### Start from

> In today's lesson, we're going to cover which holdings make the most sense in taxable, traditional, and Roth accounts, because the same investment can produce a very different after-tax result depending on where it is held.

### Cover it in this order

1. Explain the three wrappers plainly:
   - Taxable gives access and creates capital-gain and income-tax consequences along the way.
   - Traditional may give a deduction now and creates ordinary income later.
   - Roth uses after-tax money and may provide tax-free qualified withdrawals later.
2. The broad principle is that higher expected growth benefits more from stronger tax shelter, but access, custody, fees, prohibited-transaction risk, and the rest of the household plan still matter.
3. Walk through the holdings:
   - Bitcoin exposure can be split between retirement wrappers for tax shielding and self-custody for control.
   - Growth assets often benefit from Roth or traditional shelter.
   - Bonds often fit traditional accounts because the yield is otherwise taxed as ordinary income.
   - Cash usually does not need valuable Roth space.
   - Real estate has its own tax tools rather than a simple account-location answer.
4. Explain how location affects retirement withdrawals. Where the asset sits changes which tax bucket is available and when.

### Avoid

- "Put your highest-growth asset in your best shelter" as a universal command.
- Saying every Roth withdrawal is automatically tax-free.
- Implying that holding Bitcoin in a self-directed IRA has the same custody and compliance trade-offs as personal self-custody.

### Handoff

The learner writes down which holdings belong in which wrappers and why. The Module 4 walkthrough enters the full routing and account-location plan.

# Module 5 — Tax Strategy

## 5.1 · Cost basis: what you paid, and how to reconstruct it

### Start from

> In today's lesson, we're going to cover cost basis, which is the purchase history the tax plan needs before it can tell you what a future sale may actually cost.

### Cover it in this order

1. State that this module is US-specific.
2. Define cost basis as what was paid to acquire an asset, adjusted for the costs and events the tax rules include. Bitcoin is tracked in units or lots, so different purchases have different basis and holding periods.
3. Use the two-lot example to show how the same quarter-Bitcoin sale can create different gains depending on which units were identified.
4. Explain that specific identification is a record made no later than the transaction under the applicable self-custody or broker rules. It is not a choice invented after the year ends.
5. Transfers between the user's own wallets generally do not erase the tax history. The blockchain shows movement; it does not prove the original purchase price or acquisition type.
6. Reconstruct from exchange and brokerage exports, confirmations, tax files, bank statements, receipts, and wallet history. Match the transfer pairs so movement between the user's own accounts is not treated as a new purchase or sale.
7. Label each lot honestly:
   - Verified.
   - Estimated for planning.
   - Still unproven.
8. A zero-basis assumption can be a conservative planning stress test. It is not an automatic legal conclusion, and an estimated planning value is not automatically a return position.

### Handoff

Have the learner gather the records and label each material lot. The tax walkthrough imports or reconstructs the history and reviews the modeled result.

## 5.2 · Taxable, tax-deferred, and Roth: bracket windows and state taxes

### Start from

> In today's lesson, we're going to give each tax bucket a job and find the years where the household may have more control over the tax rate.

### Cover it in this order

1. Define taxable, tax-deferred, and Roth without overselling any one of them.
2. Give each a job:
   - Taxable is flexible and can fund the years before retirement-account access.
   - Traditional is useful when the deduction today is worth more than the expected future cost.
   - Roth creates long-duration tax-free potential when the qualified-distribution rules are met.
3. Find the planning window between earned income falling and Social Security, Medicare thresholds, or required distributions filling more of the return.
4. RMD age depends on the person's birth cohort. Do not memorize one age for everyone; use the current app and IRS guidance.
5. The bracket top is only the starting point. A conversion or gain can affect Social Security taxation, Marketplace credits, Medicare IRMAA, NIIT, capital-gain stacking, state tax, deductions, and credits.
6. Explain capital-gain stacking. Ordinary taxable income fills the stack first, so the household does not receive a completely separate zero-rate bucket of gains.
7. Treat state tax as a separate model. The app can compare assumptions but cannot establish legal domicile or determine every source-income rule.
8. Give the couple's roadmap: use taxable as the bridge, model gains and conversions together, check healthcare and state effects, rerun as Social Security and Medicare begin, and preserve Roth flexibility rather than spending it by default.

### Handoff

The learner writes down each account's job and the years that deserve review. The tax walkthrough models a sale and conversion as previews before anything is applied.

# Module 6 — Retirement Income

## 6.1 · Your spending, income floor, gap, and bridge years

### Start from

> In today's lesson, we're going to figure out what retirement actually costs, what income arrives without selling anything, and how much the portfolio has to cover.

### Cover it in this order

1. Build one realistic annual spending number from baseline living costs, flexible spending, healthcare, and lumpy expenses.
2. Define the income floor as reliable income arriving without a portfolio sale: Social Security, pension, verified recurring income, and part-time income the user genuinely expects.
3. Spending minus the income floor is the portfolio-funded gap. The app may call it a deficit; nothing has necessarily gone wrong.
4. Read two useful ratios: the annual gap as a percentage of the portfolio and the reserve measured in years of the gap.
5. Explain why the gap is often largest early. The paycheck ends before Social Security or another income floor begins.
6. Count both bridges:
   - Retirement to Social Security or other durable income.
   - Retirement to retirement-account access, where applicable.
7. Price the bridge, but do not pretend the simplest multiplication captures every later benefit change. Compare Social Security claiming ages against the full plan rather than repeating the default advice to always wait.
8. Build the retirement-sized reserve before the retirement date so the first drawdown does not create forced sales.

### Handoff

The learner writes down retirement spending, reliable income, the annual gap, and both bridge periods. The Module 6 walkthrough enters and tests them.

## 6.2 · Set your withdrawal order and refill rule

### Start from

> In today's lesson, we're going to decide where the retirement paycheck comes from and how the cash reserve gets refilled after it is spent.

### Cover it in this order

1. Explain the default withdrawal order as a starting point, not a rigid lifetime command.
2. Show where a strict one-account-at-a-time order breaks. Low ordinary-income years can go unused while a traditional account grows into larger later withdrawals.
3. Explain bracket-aware blending. Use some traditional money while rates are relatively low, then use taxable assets for the rest of spending. The actual split depends on the household's bracket room, basis, state, healthcare, and balances.
4. Explain that Orange Plan models bracket-aware behavior and allows custom phases. The current-year execution still needs the full tax return.
5. Draw and refill:
   - Spend the current paycheck from Reserve.
   - Bridge replenishes Reserve.
   - Legacy or other long-duration assets replenish Bridge under the saved policy.
6. Refill from strength rather than selling volatile assets simply because the calendar turned. A bad market may slow the refill; it does not quietly erase the target.
7. Tie the refill pace to the annual review and guardrail result rather than daily price movement.

### Handoff

The learner writes down the withdrawal order and refill rule. The walkthrough previews the strategy before it is applied to the plan.

## 6.3 · Guardrails: how much you can spend each year

### Start from

> In today's lesson, we're going to turn the confidence result into a spending rule the household can review once a year instead of reacting every time Bitcoin moves.

### Cover it in this order

1. Explain sequence-of-returns risk. The same average return can produce different outcomes when the bad years happen early versus late and the household is withdrawing at the same time.
2. A fixed inflation-adjusted withdrawal ignores that difference. A Bitcoin-heavy plan needs a rule that can adjust when the early sequence is weak.
3. Explain the Orange Plan bands:
   - Around 60%: lower guardrail, consider a cut toward the target.
   - Around 80%: hold and apply the normal policy.
   - Around 95%: upper guardrail, the plan may support a raise.
4. Annual changes are capped at 10% under the current policy. Explain the mechanism without presenting the exact thresholds as universal financial law.
5. Explain the choice between taking a direct cut and permanently skipping an inflation increase. Austin currently prefers taking the direct cut and allowing the future policy to continue normally.
6. A confidence score is an early warning from the modeled paths, not a grade and not proof that one adjustment rescues every failed path.
7. Use the couple's 61%, 83%, and 96% examples to show cut, hold, and raise without turning the paycheck into a market ticker.
8. Connect the same annual review to reserve refill pace.

### Handoff

Have the learner write down what a 10% cut would mean in real dollars and what expenses would change first. The walkthrough builds and previews the complete retirement paycheck.

# Module 7 — Custody

## 7.1 · Choose the custody setup that matches your stack and family

### Start from

> In today's lesson, we're going to choose the simplest custody setup that protects the amount you have and that your family can realistically recover.

### Cover it in this order

1. The four levels are an Orange Plan framework, not a Bitcoin protocol standard or a score.
2. Start with five questions: how much is at stake, who depends on it, which failure matters most, how much complexity the household will maintain, and whether another person could recover it without learning Bitcoin during a crisis.
3. Explain Level 1 as delegated institutional control. The family test is the institution's account-recovery and death-claim process, not a wallet restore.
4. Explain Level 2 as a single-signature hardware-wallet setup. The device is replaceable; the actual backup standard and any required information are what matter.
5. Explain Level 3 as additional separation, often through a passphrase or another independent arrangement. A BIP39 passphrase derives a different wallet and is not multisig.
6. Explain Level 4 as threshold signing such as 2-of-3 multisig. Keys alone are not the whole recovery package; the policy or descriptor and compatible recovery path also matter.
7. More complexity can become the largest risk. A simpler setup that is maintained and tested can be safer than a more advanced setup nobody can recover.

### Handoff

The learner writes down the level, why it fits, and the recovery test it has to pass. The custody demo and walkthrough handle the device process and no-secrets map.

## 7.2 · Set up a hardware wallet and test recovery

### Start from

> In today's lesson, we're going to cover what a hardware wallet actually does and how to prove the recovery process without making the test itself the biggest risk.

### Cover it in this order

1. The Bitcoin is not inside the device. The device protects signing keys and is replaceable.
2. Recovery depends on the actual setup and may require the backup standard, passphrase, address or script type, derivation information, or multisig policy. Do not assume every mnemonic restores every wallet on every device.
3. Buy from a source the manufacturer recognizes and follow current authenticity and firmware instructions. Never use recovery words or a PIN that arrived preprinted.
4. Record the actual backup standard. Do not simply assume every wallet uses 12 or 24 BIP39 words.
5. Test in a safer order:
   - Manufacturer backup check when available.
   - Spare compatible device or approved recovery environment with a small amount.
   - Destructive wipe-and-restore only after the backup has already been checked and the exact procedure is open.
6. Verify the intended wallet, not only that the device accepted the words. Check an expected address, fingerprint, policy, or small test transaction.
7. Confirm receiving addresses on the trusted hardware display.
8. Store recovery material offline with redundancy across independent locations. PIN, backup, and passphrase are different things.
9. Update only from official sources after the recovery path is known.

### Handoff

The learner writes down the recovery proof this setup will use. The exact device actions belong in the demo, not in generic narration.

## 7.3 · Single points of failure, account hardening, and scams

### Start from

> In today's lesson, we're going to find the one failure that could still reach the entire setup and fix the largest one first.

### Cover it in this order

1. Define a single point of failure as one person, device, backup, provider, email, phone number, or location whose loss or compromise can authorize a spend or stop recovery.
2. Do not build a list of 20 risks and fix none of them. Ask what one thing can authorize everything and what one thing can permanently block recovery.
3. Harden the primary email first because it is often the reset path for the exchanges, brokerages, banks, and cloud accounts.
4. Prefer phishing-resistant authentication when supported. A hardware key or passkey can be stronger than a code; an authenticator app is generally stronger than SMS, but a typed code can still be phished.
5. Harden each custodial account: unique password, strongest practical authentication, offline recovery codes, withdrawal controls when offered, active-session review, and a tested recovery path.
6. Check the support-reset process. Strong login security does not help if support will reset the account through weak identity verification.
7. Scams use urgency and authority. Stop, close the message, navigate through a known channel, verify separately, and never reveal recovery material.
8. Limit who knows the amount, locations, and exact setup. The family needs the process; not everyone needs every secret.

### Handoff

The learner writes down the largest remaining failure and one dated fix. The custody demo and walkthrough implement the account hardening and custody map.

# Module 8 — Estate and Inheritance

## 8.1 · The executor, the four legal documents, and choosing an estate attorney

### Start from

> In today's lesson, we're going to put the legal and family process around the custody setup so the right person has authority and knows where to begin.

### Cover it in this order

1. State that the module is US-specific and state-specific. The course can explain the jobs; a state-licensed attorney drafts the actual documents.
2. A will usually nominates an executor. The court appointment generally creates the authority to act after death. No will does not mean nobody can ever act; it means state defaults and the court choose more of the process.
3. Explain the four document jobs:
   - Will: probate assets, executor nomination, and possible guardian nomination.
   - Financial power of attorney: authority during life, generally ending at death.
   - Healthcare directive: medical decisions during incapacity.
   - Beneficiary, transfer, and digital-access records: non-probate transfers and the authority providers and fiduciaries may need.
4. A beneficiary designation may generally control the covered account, but plan terms, spousal rules, state law, and an invalid or outdated form can change the result. Orange Plan records intent; the provider holds the binding record.
5. Executor is a real job. Ask the person first. They need the attorney, tax professional, legal documents, no-secrets process map, and first steps. They do not automatically need every signing secret.
6. Legal authority and technical control are different. A person may be legally authorized but unable to sign; another may hold signing material without authority to use it. The estate and custody plans have to align.
7. Give the attorney a focused list: which assets pass under which mechanism, digital-asset authority during incapacity and death, state signing rules, concentrated Bitcoin language, and coordination of executor, trustee, heir, and key holders.
8. The Executor Packet is an operational briefing. It does not appoint anyone or override a legal document.

### Handoff

The learner writes down the legal roles, missing documents, and attorney questions. The estate walkthrough records completion without storing legal documents or secrets in the app.

## 8.2 · Split access: dual control and redundancy

### Start from

> In today's lesson, we're going to answer two different questions: can one person spend alone, and can one loss permanently stop recovery?

### Cover it in this order

1. Dual control asks whether one person or one item is enough to spend. Redundancy asks whether one loss is enough to block recovery.
2. A setup can pass one and fail the other. Several copies of one seed improve location redundancy but create several complete spending copies.
3. A mnemonic and passphrase stored apart can create two-part operational access, but it is not on-chain multisig and losing either may stop recovery.
4. A correctly documented 2-of-3 multisig can pass both tests when one key cannot spend, one key can be lost, and the policy or descriptor is recoverable.
5. Do not invent one universal Level 2 family arrangement. Write down who can technically recover, who is legally authorized, how redundant copies are protected, whether one-person spending is accepted, and what would justify a future upgrade.
6. Executor, trustee, heir, guardian, provider, and key holder are different roles. The legal map and key map must agree.
7. The family meeting teaches who calls whom, where the legal and no-secrets process documents live, which actions require another person, and what nobody should reveal or improvise.

### Handoff

The learner writes down whether the current setup passes each test and which failure is deliberately accepted. The estate walkthrough records the level and recovery status without storing secrets.

## 8.3 · The heir letter and the dead man's switch

### Start from

> In today's lesson, we're going to write the first page the family reads and make sure it starts the process without containing the secrets.

### Cover it in this order

1. The first line says who to call. The second line says what not to do.
2. The letter can name the existence of Bitcoin, legal and professional contacts, where the legal documents and no-secrets map are kept, the order of operations, provider claim processes, and how to verify a helper.
3. The letter does not contain wallet backup words, private keys, passphrases, PINs, passwords, full account numbers, exact secret locations, or enough detail to bypass the control structure.
4. Separate the documents:
   - Heir letter: where to begin.
   - Custody map: setup, roles, contacts, and dependencies without the secrets.
   - Executor packet: legal and tax order.
   - Controlled recovery package: wallet-specific details appropriate to the setup.
5. A dead man's switch can deliver the letter or tell the family where the process documents are. It should never deliver signing secrets.
6. Test for false triggers, spam, changed contact information, service failure, and the possibility that Local Only mode needs a manual delivery process.
7. The message does not create legal authority to access an account. The attorney still needs to address the relevant digital-asset and communication authority.

### Handoff

The learner drafts the first two lines, chooses the recipient, and writes down the manual backup delivery process. The walkthrough builds and tests the heir-letter workflow.

## 8.4 · Insurance: term life, disability, umbrella, and when to stop

### Start from

> In today's lesson, we're going to identify which risks the household cannot absorb yet and which insurance questions are worth taking to a licensed reviewer.

### Cover it in this order

1. Insurance transfers a loss the household is not willing or able to absorb. Start with the event, size and duration of the loss, resources already available, and what the actual contract promises.
2. Life insurance protects people whose plan depends on the insured person's income or work. A rough need can start from the survivor shortfall and years of dependence, but also include debts, childcare, education, survivor income, liquidity, inflation, and existing coverage.
3. Term and permanent are categories, not one product each. Austin may prefer term for a temporary household need, but do not describe every permanent policy as bad savings or claim Bitcoin replaces every insurance purpose.
4. Disability insurance protects earned income while the person is alive. Read the actual benefit cap, tax treatment, elimination period, benefit period, occupation definition, offsets, exclusions, and residual-disability rules. Compare the after-tax benefit with bare-bones spending.
5. Umbrella coverage generally adds excess liability above required underlying limits, subject to the policy's exclusions. Do not quote a universal cost or claim it covers every loss.
6. Long-term-care planning depends on health, family support, assets available to self-fund, benefit triggers, care preferences, inflation protection, and the current available products. "Not now" can be a finished answer with a dated review trigger.
7. Health-sharing is not insurance and belongs in a healthcare comparison, not as a contract substitute.
8. Coverage can fall when the exposure falls, but do not cancel or replace an existing policy until the replacement and consequences are understood.

### Handoff

The learner writes down which gaps need a current quote or contract review and which risks the household is retaining. The estate walkthrough records the unresolved protection items.

# Module 9 — Run, Maintain, Test, and Read the Plan

## 9.1 · Keep the plan current: the monthly pass and annual review

### Start from

> In today's lesson, we're going to set a short monthly routine for keeping the facts current and one annual review for the decisions that actually deserve more time.

### Cover it in this order

1. A review updates what changed. A rebuild reopens decisions every time the price moves and becomes too heavy to maintain.
2. Separate data drift from plan drift:
   - Data drift: income, spending, balances, accounts, and transactions become stale.
   - Plan drift: an allocation, debt job, reserve, custody setup, or estate decision no longer fits the household.
3. The monthly pass is about five minutes and catches data drift. The annual review is the deeper lap that catches plan drift.
4. Update the facts before evaluating the strategy. Stale numbers produce confident but wrong answers.
5. Keep the monthly pass narrow: transactions, meaningful income or spending changes, life events, and a quick check that assumptions or targets did not deliberately change.
6. Distinguish a new baseline from a one-off. A raise is a new baseline. One expensive restaurant month is not. Repeated drift in the same direction may be.
7. Price action, news, or a desire for a nicer result does not belong in the baseline assumptions.
8. Run the annual review in a month that leaves time for tax actions before year-end. The current course uses November.
9. Limit the output to one to three finishable actions. A huge backlog is what makes the review stop happening.

### Correct one stale line

Do not say missing records automatically make legal basis zero. Say the basis remains unresolved and a zero-basis assumption may be used only as a conservative planning stress test until the evidence and filing position are reviewed.

### Handoff

The learner schedules a recurring monthly pass and the November annual review and writes down what will not be reopened monthly. The final walkthrough runs the completed process.

## 9.2 · Test a decision and read the finished plan

### Start from

> In today's lesson, we're going to test a decision without changing the real plan, and then read the finished report in an order that makes the numbers useful.

### Cover it in this order

1. Re-establish the distinction:
   - Life event: something the user genuinely expects and needs in the baseline.
   - Scenario: a question, choice, or stress test kept separate from the baseline.
2. Ask one test: am I telling the plan what is true, or asking the plan a question?
3. Separate stress tests from choices. A stress test asks whether the plan survives something. A choice compares two futures the household could deliberately select.
4. Change one variable at a time so the result teaches something. Test the combined case only after the individual effects are understood.
5. Read the comparison as a trade-off, not a prettier number. An earlier date at much lower confidence is not automatically a better result. Small differences may be projection noise rather than a life-changing signal.
6. When a scenario becomes a real decision, update the baseline. Do not leave an adopted decision living only in Scenarios.
7. Keep two useful recurring stress tests: a major drawdown early in retirement and weaker Bitcoin returns than the baseline assumes.
8. Read the finished report in four questions:
   - Where are we today?
   - Where are we headed?
   - What could break the plan?
   - What are the one to three actions?
9. Read retirement date and confidence together. Confirm the current position before trusting trajectory; then read the risk sections and spending range.
10. Save one PDF after the annual review each year. The value appears when two reports can be compared using the same lines rather than comparing only Bitcoin price or net worth.
11. Use the report as a family and professional handoff: spouse, CPA, and attorney can start from the same facts without receiving app credentials.

### Handoff

The final walkthrough completes Build Your Plan, runs the 1,000-path confidence check, sets the confidence target, builds one real scenario, reads the report, and saves the PDF.
